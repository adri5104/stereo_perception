\chapter{Introduction}\label{chap:Introduction}

Autonomous vehicles are quickly heading towards a widespread deployment, with 
the potential to significantly improve traffic safety, alleviate congestion, 
and increase overall transportation efficiency. 

At the heart of this transformation lies the ability to perceive the surrounding environment with 
high accuracy and robustness. Perception systems enable vehicles to detect 
obstacles, understand the behavior of other traffic agents, and understand 
the overall context of the driving scene. However, the real world is dynamic and 
often unstructured, presenting substantial challenges to perception algorithms.

Failures in perception can lead to disengagements, situations in which the 
automated driving system can no longer identify a safe course of action and 
must transfer control to a human operator. These events 
typically occur outside the optimal operating conditions of the system, and are 
especially critical. Even in such scenarios, the vehicle must maintain a  
of situational awareness to support fallback strategies or assist the operator 
in resolving the situation safely.

Current state-of-the-art perception systems are often learning-based, 
with deep neural networks trained on large annotated datasets. These methods 
perform well in structured, well-represented environments but struggle to 
generalize to rare or previously unseen scenarios. Their lack of transparency 
also raises concerns about interpretability and systematic validation, key 
requirements in safety-critical applications.

To address these limitations, this thesis explores a complementary, deterministic 
approach using stereo camera information. The goal is to design and implement a 
perception system capable of estimating the position and motion of surrounding 
objects without reliance on prior training or semantic annotations. By focusing 
on geometric cues such as disparity and optical flow, the system aims to achieve 
robust performance across diverse and challenging environments.

The system processes synchronized stereo images from a vehicle mounted camera 
setup to produce 6D motion estimates: three dimensional positions and the corresponging
velocities of selected scene points. A Kalman filter-based framework, inspired by the 
Daimler 6D Vision approach, integrates depth, optical flow, and ego-motion 
information to compute these estimates. The resulting motion field is clustered 
using the \gls{DBSCAN} algorithm to identify coherent moving objects.

To assess potential hazards, a criticality evaluation module calculates safety 
metrics based on predicted trajectories of both the ego-vehicle and the detected 
objects. One key metric, \gls{TTC}, provides insight into the 
urgency of evasive actions. These evaluations are performed in real time to 
support both autonomous fallback strategies and teleoperated decision-making.

This work builds on previous research at the Chair of Automotive Technology of the Technical University of Munich, 
which investigated stereo vision and deterministic motion estimation. The 
present study focuses on integrating these methods into a modular, reusable 
software component. A major contribution is the development of an independent 
\gls{ROS2} service, designed for seamless integration with the EDGAR research vehicle and 
adaptability to other platforms.

The system is implemented in C++, with performance critical components 
accelerated using \gls{CUDA} and parallelized via OpenMP. This ensures 
that real-time constraints are met while maintaining scalability and robustness. 
Each major function, depth estimation, optical flow, Kalman filtering, clustering, 
and criticality assessment, is encapsulated in its own \gls{ROS2} package, supporting modular 
development, testing, and integration. The use of Docker for containerization 
ensures portability and simplifies deployment across heterogeneous systems.

The system architecture consists of two main processing pipelines:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Perception pipeline:} Combines stereo vision, visual odometry, and 
  deterministic motion estimation to infer 6D trajectories of scene points. 
  Moving objects are identified by clustering these trajectories based on spatial 
  and motion consistency.

  \item \textbf{Criticality assessment pipeline:} Analyzes interactions between 
  the ego-vehicle and detected objects to compute risk-related metrics that 
  support fallback decisions and driver assistance.
\end{itemize}


The evaluation of the system is structured in three phases. First, the stereo
camera hardware is tested and calibrated to ensure suitability for accurate 6D
motion estimation. If necessary, alternative hardware is assessed and integrated.
Second, the computational performance of the full perception and criticality
pipeline is benchmarked to verify real-time capabilities.
Finally, the system is deployed on the EDGAR research vehicle for real-world
validation, where its outputs are compared against the existing Autowarelearning-based perception stack to
assess robustness and reliability. All experiments aim to verify compliance with
the functional requirements defined in Chapter~\ref{sec:RequirementsForTheThesis},
including stereo-only sensing, semantic independence, modularity, real-time
operation, and robustness under occlusion.

In summary, this thesis presents a deterministic, modular, and real-time capable 
perception system for automated driving. By leveraging stereo vision and geometric 
algorithms, the system enhances environmental understanding in situations where 
deep learning methods may fall short. Its deployment as a ROS2 service, optimized 
for performance and portability, enables easy integration into diverse research 
and development workflows.

Finally, with regard to the structure of this document,it is organized 
into the following chapters. Chapter~\ref{chap:StateOfTheArt} surveys existing 
environment perception systems for automated driving, including \gls{LiDAR}, \gls{RADAR}, 
and image based methods. It further reviews algorithms for detecting moving 
objects, distinguishes learning-based from deterministic approaches, and 
summarizes relevant research conducted at the Chair of Automotive Technology. 
Chapter~\ref{chap:MethodsAndImplementation} presents the design and 
implementation of the proposed system, separating the perception and criticality 
pipelines into distinct modules. Chapter~\ref{chap:ExperimentsAndResults} 
describes the evaluation process, including both component-level tests and 
experiments conducted on the EDGAR research vehicle. 
Chapter~\ref{chap:SummaryAndConclusion} concludes the thesis by 
summarizing the main contributions and outlining future work.

\chapter{State of the Art}\label{chap:StateOfTheArt}

Autonomous and automated vehicles must have a comprehesive and accurate understanding of their surroundings at 
all times to ensure safe and reliable operation. This process, known as environment perception, is responsible
for detecting, identifying, and tracking objects such as other vehicles, cyclists, pedestrians or other
road elements. The quality of the measurement of the state of this objects directly influence the vehicle's
ability to make safe and informed driving decisions. 

Failures in the perception can lead to hazarous situations, such as collisions or unexpected navigation decisions.
This is particularly critical in high-speed scenarios or complex urban environments, where is a high density of
high dynamic objects which make more complex the operation in real time. 

A robust perception system must provide the following capabilities:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Object detection:} Identify and locate objects in the vehicle's surroundings, such as vehicles, pedestrians, cyclists, or other road elements.
  \item \textbf{Motion estimation:} Determining the position and velocity of moving objects
  \item \textbf{Scene understanding:} Classifiying the detected objects and assessing their criticality for the vehicle's navigation
  \item \textbf{Reliability in edge cases:} Ensuring correct operation in complex and unforeseen scenarios, such as occlusions, adverse weather conditions, or sensor failures.
  \item \textbf{Real-time operation:} Providing timely and accurate information to the vehicle's control system to make informed driving decisions.
\end{enumerate}

While the perception concept includes different aspects, this work will focus specifically on the detection of 
moving object in the vehicle's surroundings and the estimation of their 6D motion state (position and velocity) 
using a deterministic approach. In the following sections, we will provide an overview of the state-of-the-art of
the different sensors and algorithms used in the perception systems of autonomous vehicles, focusing on the deterministic image
based approaches.

\section{Perception Systems for Autonomous Vehicles}\label{sec:PerceptionSystemsForAutonomousVehicles}

Perception systems are a core component of autonomous and automated vehicles. They enable the continuous 
interpretation of the environment by converting raw sensor data into structured information, which is then used by planning and control modules. The accuracy, reliability, and completeness of this 
information directly influence the vehicle's ability to operate safely and efficiently in various driving 
conditions~\cite{autonomous_vehicles_perception_survey}.

To obtain a comprehensive representation of the surrounding environment, modern autonomous systems integrate 
data from multiple sensor modalities. These include LiDAR, RADAR, and camera systems, each offering distinct 
capabilities and constraints. The fusion of these sensors allows the vehicle to mitigate individual 
sensor limitations and achieve a more robust perception. However, understanding the characteristics 
of each sensor independently remains essential, especially in system design or in the development 
of fallback strategies based on deterministic algorithms.

The following subsections provide an overview of the most common sensor types used in autonomous 
vehicles, including their working principles, strengths, and limitations. This review lays the 
foundation for the sensor selection and processing strategies discussed later in this work.

\subsubsection{LiDAR-Based Perception}

LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging) is widely employed in automotive perception for its ability 
to generate dense and geometrically accurate 3D representations of the environment 
~\cite{autonomous_vehicles_perception_survey}. By measuring the round-trip time of emitted laser pulses, 
LiDAR produces a point cloud that encodes spatial structure with high resolution.

This technology is particularly effective in tasks such as object detection, 3D mapping, lane boundary 
identification, and free-space estimation. Its ability to provide absolute distance measurements makes 
it well suited for geometric computations without dependence on visual features. Automotive companies 
including BMW~\cite{lidarbased_bmw}, Mercedes-Benz~\cite{lidarbased_mercedes}, and Waymo~\cite{lidarbased_waymo} 
incorporate LiDAR in their perception stacks, particularly for high-level automation.

\paragraph{Working principle}

A LiDAR unit emits a sequence of laser pulses into the environment and detects the reflected signals 
using photodetectors. By calculating the time interval between emission and reception, and knowing 
the speed of light, the distance to each object surface is computed~\cite{lidarbased_review}. Modern 
automotive LiDARs rotate or scan across multiple directions to generate a full 3D point cloud of the 
scene.

\paragraph{Advantages and Limitations}

The main advantage of LiDAR is its precise depth sensing and high spatial resolution. This allows 
robust obstacle detection independent of ambient light conditions. However, LiDAR is sensitive to 
weather disturbances such as rain, fog, or snow~\cite{lidarAdverseWeather}, where light scattering can degrade data quality. 
In addition, LiDAR devices typically incur higher costs than camera or radar systems. Although prices are decreasing, 
LiDAR adoption in production vehicles remains limited to higher-end or research platforms.

\subsubsection{Radar-Based Perception}

Radar (Radio Detection and Ranging) sensors use electromagnetic waves in the radio frequency spectrum 
to detect objects and estimate their distance and relative velocity. These sensors have long been 
used in automotive systems, particularly for functions such as adaptive cruise control and collision 
avoidance.

\paragraph{Working principle}

Radar sensors emit radio signals that reflect off surfaces in the environment. The time delay 
between emission and reception, combined with the known wave propagation speed, enables the 
computation of object distances. The Doppler shift in the frequency of the reflected waves 
further allows the estimation of relative velocities~\cite{radarbased_velocity}. 

\paragraph{Advantages and Limitations}

Radar systems are robust under adverse environmental conditions. Their signals are less affected 
by rain, fog, or dust, making them a reliable source of detection even in degraded visual 
environments~\cite{autonomous_vehicles_perception_survey, radarbased_weather}. Moreover, 
their ability to directly measure relative velocity provides useful dynamic information. 

However, the spatial resolution of radar is lower than that of LiDAR or cameras, which can 
complicate object classification or detailed localization. Additionally, radar signals may 
produce multipath effects or suffer from interference in complex environments.

\subsubsection{Image-Based Perception}

Camera-based systems provide rich visual information that can be used for a variety of 
perception tasks, including classification, semantic segmentation, visual odometry, and depth estimation.
They are an essential part of most autonomous driving stacks and, in some designs, serve as the 
primary perception modality, as exemplified by the Autopilot system of Tesla~\cite{tesla_autopilot}.

\paragraph{Monocular vision}

Monocular setups involve a single camera capturing 2D projections of the environment. These systems 
are effective for detecting traffic signs, identifying objects, and inferring semantic information. 
They benefit from low cost and ease of integration but have limited capacity to infer depth without 
additional cues or motion-based estimation methods~\cite{autonomous_vehicles_perception_survey}.

\paragraph{Stereo vision}

Stereo camera systems use two synchronized cameras mounted at a known baseline. By applying geometric 
constraints from epipolar geometry and the pinhole camera model~\cite{pin_hole_camera_model}, the 
system computes disparity maps that can be converted into depth information. This enables passive 
3D reconstruction without active illumination, making stereo vision particularly attractive for 
dense, low-cost depth estimation in structured environments.

\paragraph{Advantages and Limitations}

Image-based perception provides access to texture, color, and appearance features, allowing detailed 
scene understanding. Cameras are compact, power-efficient, and cheap relative to other sensors. 
Nonetheless, their performance is susceptible to lighting variations, shadows, glare, and occlusions. 
Depth estimation from stereo vision requires careful calibration and performs poorly in textureless 
regions or when the baseline is insufficient. Computational demands can also be significant, 
particularly when processing high-resolution frames or running complex algorithms in real time.

\section{Algorithms for Moving Object Detection}\label{sec:AlgorithmsForMovingObjectDetection}
\label{sec:algorithms_for_moving_object_detection}

Beyond sensor selection, the effectiveness of a perception system depends heavily on the algorithms 
used to process the raw sensor data. These algorithms are 
responsible for interpreting raw measurements to estimate the presence, position, and motion of 
objects relevant to driving decisions.

Moving object detection is a fundamental task in autonomous vehicles, as it enables the system 
to anticipate changes in the environment and plan appropriate maneuvers. The algorithms designed 
for this task can generally be classified into two broad categories: learning-based approaches 
and deterministic approaches. The former relies on data-driven models, while the latter is based 
on physical and geometric principles. Each paradigm has its strengths and limitations, and they 
often complement each other in hybrid systems.

The following sections provide an overview of both categories, highlighting representative methods, 
their operational characteristics, and their relevance to the goals of this thesis.

\subsubsection{Learning-Based Approaches}
\label{sec:learning_based_approaches}

Learning-based approaches have become predominant in many domains of computer vision, including 
object detection, tracking, and scene understanding. These methods use models such as 
\gls{CNN}, \gls{RNN}, and transformer-based 
architectures to learn mappings from raw sensor inputs to high-level perception outputs.

In the context of moving object detection, learning-based methods typically combine object detection 
with instance tracking over time. Networks are trained end-to-end to predict object categories, 
bounding boxes, and motion parameters. For example, approaches such as CenterTrack~\cite{centerTrack} 
and TrackRCNN~\cite{trackRCNN} integrate detection and tracking into a unified framework. Some methods 
extend this further to 3D detection using point cloud data or camera-LiDAR fusion, as explored 
in works like PointRCNN~\cite{pointRCNN} and DeepFusion~\cite{deepFusion}.

A comprehensive review of perception techniques based on deep learning is provided by Wen and Jo~\cite{learning_based_perception_survey}, where architectures are categorized by input modality, 
task type, and application scope. A simplified summary of these methods is shown in 
Figure~\ref{fig:learning_based_methods}.

\begin{figure}[!h]
  \centering

  \caption{Learning-based perception methods. Source:~\cite{learning_based_perception_survey}}\label{fig:learning_based_methods}
\end{figure}

Despite their high accuracy and generalization across many scenarios, learning-based methods 
present important challenges. They require large volumes of annotated data for training and 
validation, which is costly and time-consuming to obtain, especially for edge-case scenarios 
such as rare weather events, unusual object classes, or emergency maneuvers. Additionally, the 
black-box nature of neural networks complicates formal verification and interpretability, which 
are essential for safety critical applications such as automated driving.

In unstructured or previously unseen environments, these models may fail to generalize correctly. 
This limitation motivates the use of complementary, model-based approaches, especially as a 
fallback or redundancy mechanism in high-assurance systems.

\subsubsection{Deterministic Approaches}
\label{sec:deterministic_approaches}

Deterministic approaches rely on physically grounded models and algorithms to estimate object motion 
from sensor data. They do not require training and are typically more interpretable, making them 
suitable for applications where transparency and real-time guarantees are essential.

\paragraph{Optical Flow}

Optical flow refers to the apparent motion of image brightness patterns between consecutive frames. 
By assuming brightness constancy and small motion between frames, optical flow algorithms estimate 
the displacement vector for each pixel or image region. This flow field encodes both motion direction 
and magnitude, which can be used to infer object motion relative to the camera.

Classic methods for optical flow computation include:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Lucas\-Kanade}~\cite{opt_flw_lukas_canade}, which estimates flow using local patches and least-squares optimization,
  \item \textbf{Horn\-Schunck}~\cite{opt_flw_schunck}, which introduces a global smoothness constraint on the flow field.
\end{itemize}


Optical flow is often used in combination with depth or disparity information to project 2D motion 
into 3D space. When paired with stereo cameras, it can provide estimates of object motion 
in the coordinate frame of the vehicle.

\paragraph{6D Vision}\label{sec:6D_Vision}

The 6D Vision framework, introduced by Franke et al.~\cite{6d_vision_daimler}, is a deterministic 
method for jointly estimating the 3D position and velocity of points in a scene using stereo vision 
and optical flow. Unlike learning-based methods, this approach does not rely on semantic segmentation 
or object-specific models. Instead, it directly tracks sparse 3D points across time and estimates 
their motion using filtering techniques.

The key components of 6D Vision are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Stereo disparity estimation:} Corresponding points are matched between left and right stereo images to estimate depth through triangulation.
  \item \textbf{Optical flow tracking:} Temporal correspondences are computed using optical flow between successive frames.
  \item \textbf{Kalman filtering:} Each tracked point is associated with a state vector comprising position and velocity. Kalman filters are used to predict and update this state over time, taking into account sensor noise and model uncertainty.
\end{enumerate}

The result is a sparse but dynamic representation of the scene, where each tracked point has an associated 6D state. 
These points can then be grouped into moving objects through clustering techniques such as \gls{DBSCAN}, enabling object-level 
motion understanding without the need for prior object models.

Because it is model-free and data-independent, 6D Vision is especially useful in scenarios where learning-based 
systems may fail due to insufficient training data or degraded input quality. However, the method requires 
accurate calibration and careful handling of uncertainty to remain robust under varying conditions.

In this thesis, we follow the principles of 6D Vision and extend them into a modular and real-time 
ROS2-based implementation. The proposed system uses stereo camera input to generate a dynamic 3D 
representation of the environment, which serves as a basis for object detection and criticality analysis. 
The deterministic nature of the approach ensures transparency and complements learning-based 
perception stacks, contributing to improved redundancy and safety in autonomous driving.

\subsection{EDGAR Research Vehicle}

The EDGAR (Excellent Driving Garching) research vehicle is a modular testbed 
developed at the Institute of Automotive Technology (FTM) at TUM~\cite{edgar_hardware}. It is designed to support the development, integration, 
and evaluation of autonomous driving software in real-world conditions.

The platform is based on a Volkswagen T7 Multivan 1.4 eHybrid. This vehicle 
offers sufficient electrical power and internal space for mounting computing 
units and sensor interfaces. It retains certified drive-by-wire functionality, 
which allows autonomous control over steering, throttle, and braking while 
preserving manual override.

EDGAR features a multi-modal sensor suite, including:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Cameras (mono, stereo, depth)} for perception and teleoperation
  \item \textbf{LiDARs (short and long range)} for 3D environment modeling
  \item \textbf{Radars} for velocity estimation in all weather conditions
  \item \textbf{GNSS/IMU} for localization and motion estimation
  \item \textbf{Microphones} for ambient sound detection
\end{itemize}

The sensors are mounted with known extrinsics and stored in a digital twin 
model, enabling reproducibility in simulation and real-world testing. An

\section{Stereo Camera Technology}\label{sec:StereoCameraTechnology}

This section reviews stereo vision technologies relevant to autonomous driving
applications. Stereo cameras provide dense depth estimation, enabling motion
perception, obstacle detection, and environment reconstruction without active
range sensing. While early systems such as 6D Vision of Daimler relied on passive
stereo only~\cite{6d_vision_daimler}, modern cameras integrate active lighting,
neural inference, and time synchronization for robust deployment.

The classification used here distinguishes passive stereo, active infrared
stereo, laser-structured light, and smart stereo systems with onboard
processing. The overview is based on the ROS2-Industrial survey
~\cite{rosindustrialCameraSurvey}, a general technology review~\cite{stereoCameraTech},
and recent empirical studies in robotics and autonomous navigation.

\subsection{Passive Stereo Vision}\label{sec:PassiveStereoVision}

Passive stereo systems compute depth from disparities between synchronized
images under ambient illumination. They are well-suited for outdoor driving
scenarios, where natural texture is often abundant.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{MultiSense S30}: A rugged stereo camera with a 30\,cm baseline,
  IP68 enclosure, and FPGA-based depth processing. It has been used in visual
  odometry pipelines for outdoor navigation, demonstrating high robustness to
  scene complexity and lighting variations~\cite{paperCarnegie}.

  \item \textbf{Luxonis OAK-D}: A lightweight smart camera integrating stereo
  depth with neural inference on-chip. In~\cite{paperLuxonis}, it was used for
  real-time gate detection and navigation in autonomous drones, showing
  potential for embedded vehicle perception tasks without relying on external
  GPUs.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Active Infrared Stereo}\label{sec:ActiveInfraredStereo}

Active infrared stereo cameras enhance depth estimation in low-texture scenes
by projecting structured light patterns. They are effective indoors or under
controlled illumination, but their performance may degrade under direct sunlight.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Intel RealSense D455}: Incorporates a global shutter stereo pair
  and infrared projector. It supports depth estimation up to 10\,m. Its
  suitability for \gls{SLAM} was evaluated in~\cite{paperIntel}, which found that
  depth accuracy decreases significantly with distance and oblique incidence,
  though it remains competitive in short-range indoor mapping.

  \item \textbf{Luxonis OAK-D Pro}: Combines active stereo with onboard AI. In
  comparative benchmarks~\cite{paperStereoComparison}, it performed reliably
  in low-light and textureless environments, offering integrated person
  detection and object tracking capabilities.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Laser-Structured Stereo}\label{sec:LaserStructuredStereo}

Laser-structured stereo systems project high-contrast patterns to improve
depth estimation. These systems are often used in industrial setups where
illumination can be controlled, and where high accuracy is required.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Zivid 2+}: A high-resolution RGB-D camera employing structured
  laser light. Its depth noise and uncertainty were characterized in~\cite{paperZivid}, where a model-based compensation scheme improved the
  performance of 3D reconstruction pipelines. Although designed for industrial
  robotics, its accuracy and noise profile make it a candidate for
  infrastructure inspection in autonomous driving.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Smart Stereo Cameras with Onboard Processing}\label{sec:SmartStereoCameras}

Smart stereo cameras integrate processing units for depth and AI tasks,
reducing bandwidth and computation load on host systems. These sensors are
increasingly used in embedded perception stacks for autonomous platforms.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Stereolabs ZED 2}: Provides real-time stereo depth and inertial
  tracking. In~\cite{paperZed}, it was used to detect and track trees from a
  mobile platform in unstructured outdoor environments. Dense stereo point
  clouds were processed into pseudo-LiDAR for object-level reasoning, showing
  applicability in naturalistic driving settings such as forests or parks.

  \item \textbf{Luxonis OAK-D}: Reappears here due to its integrated VPU, which
  supports neural model inference and stereo depth estimation in real time.
  Its use in closed-loop autonomous navigation~\cite{paperLuxonis} exemplifies
  its relevance for perception systems with limited computational budgets.
\end{itemize}

Stereo vision is employed as the primary sensing modality for motion estimation
in this work. The Framos D455e, an active stereo camera with infrared projection,
is currently deployed on the EDGAR platform. Its real-time
capabilities align with the requirements of this project. 
Alternative sensor options and their impact on performance are
discussed in Section~\ref{sec:CameraHardware}.

\section{Requirements for the Thesis}\label{sec:RequirementsForTheThesis}

Robust detection of dynamic objects is a fundamental requirement in automated
driving, as it directly affects the capability of the vehicle to avoid hazards and
make safe navigation decisions. While deep learning-based systems currently
dominate commercial perception stacks, their depencency on extensive labeled
datasets, limited generalization in rare scenarios, and lack of transparency
introduce challenges in safety-critical contexts. This has led to increased
interest in deterministic alternatives, which leverage geometric and physical
priors to infer motion from raw sensor data without the need for training.

Among these, LiDAR-based methods have demonstrated strong performance due to
their accurate depth estimation. Voxel-based segmentation approaches such as~\cite{asvadi_3d_2016} and mapless, distortion-compensated techniques like~\cite{yoon_mapless_2018} show that dynamic object detection can be performed
in real time using 3D geometry alone. However, LiDAR sensors remain expensive,
weather-sensitive, and difficult to integrate into embedded platforms. These
limitations motivate the use of stereo vision, which provides dense spatial
information at significantly lower cost and system complexity.

Camera-based deterministic methods have emerged as a promising alternative.
In~\cite{eppenberger_leveraging_2020}, a stereo system detects and tracks
moving obstacles in real time using sparse point clouds and inter-frame
clustering. The method achieves high tracking accuracy on embedded hardware.
Other works such as~\cite{ess_moving_2009} extend this concept to dynamic
pedestrian environments, while~\cite{shi_dynamic_2018} proposes efficient
filtering of dynamic points to support consistent 3D mapping. Although these
methods produce promising results, many rely on heuristic motion classification
or operate in simplified 2D spaces.

The 6D Vision framework, described in Section~\ref{sec:6D_Vision}, provides a
more principled approach. It combines stereo disparity and optical flow to
estimate the full 3D position and velocity of scene points. This method tracks
sparse features over time and uses Kalman filters to estimate their
trajectories, enabling object-agnostic motion reasoning through recursive
estimation.

This thesis adopts and modernizes the 6D Vision paradigm, introducing updates
in both algorithmic design and system-level implementation. The developed
system is implemented entirely in C++ and ROS2 and designed for deployment on
the EDGAR research vehicle. A key novelty is the full modularization of the
pipeline into a reusable and scalable ROS2 service. Each processing stage: depth
estimation, optical flow, motion tracking, object clustering, and risk
assessment, is encapsulated as an independent component, allowing flexible
testing, extension, and integration with additional modules.

In contrast to previous work at the FTM~\cite{previous_tfm_bauer,
previous_tfm_gpu_implementation, previous_tfm_pastor}, which used ADTF or
partial ROS based implementations with limited deployment, this thesis
delivers a fully containerized, real-time capable perception system. The system
is tested under real-world conditions using the Framos D455e stereo camera and
the infrastructure of the EDGAR vehicle. Furthermore, a new
egomotion compensation module is introduced to enable consistent alignment to
the world frame, separating ego movement from external object motion.

Another important contribution is the integration of a motion-based clustering
step, which aggregates coherent point trajectories into object-level
representations. These clusters form the basis for the computation of runtime
criticality metrics, a novel extension not considered in previous FTM work.
The criticality module computes indicators such as Time-to-Collision (TTC) by
considering the relative velocity and distance between moving obstacles and the
ego trajectory. This enables quantitative risk assessment in
teleoperated fallback scenarios, where robust scene understanding is required
despite degraded sensing or connectivity or even an additional security layer when driving
autonomously.

To meet the outlined goals and ensure reliable operation in real-world
conditions, the system must fulfill the following functional and technical
requirements:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Real-time performance:} The system must maintain a minimum
processing frequency of 10\,Hz to satisfy latency constraints of autonomous
vehicle control loops. This requirement aligns with timing guidelines reported
in safety-critical perception pipelines~\cite{req_freq}. To meet this
constraint under embedded hardware limitations, the implementation incorporates
gpu acceleration and multithreadeding for computationally intensive components.

  \item \textbf{Stereo‑only sensing:} Exclusive use of stereo vision to reduce
    system complexity and avoid reliance on expensive lidar sensors.
    Previous research has demonstrated that stereo-only pipelines can reliably
    perform dynamic obstacle detection and scene understanding without requiring
    additional sensor modalities~\cite{eppenberger_leveraging_2020, ess_moving_2009}. This design decision is also consistent with
    deployment constraints on the EDGAR platform, where the total sensor budget was
    limited to approximately €3,000.

  \item \textbf{Semantic independence:} Object detection must operate without
    class labels or training data, enabling generalization to unknown object types
    and rare scenarios. This is particularly important for fallback perception in
    safety-critical applications, where learning-based methods may fail due to
    dataset bias or degraded inputs~\cite{learning_based_perception_survey}.
    As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:AlgorithmsForMovingObjectDetection}, this
    thesis adopts a deterministic paradigm inspired by 6D Vision~\cite{6d_vision_daimler},
    where dynamic scene points are tracked independently of semantic categories.
    This approach provides interpretable motion reasoning and increases robustness
    in scenarios where learned models may be unreliable.

  \item \textbf{Egomotion compensation:} Accurate transformation of observations
    into a consistent reference frame is essential for distinguishing between
    static and dynamic objects. Without compensating for the motion of the ego
    vehicle, apparent motion induced by the camera itself would confound motion
    estimation and object tracking~\cite{6d_vision_detailed}.
  \item \textbf{6D output:} The system must estimate both 3D position and
    velocity for each observed point or object to enable a dynamic representation
    of the environment. This enriched state information is essential for
    differentiating between moving and stationary objects, predicting future
    trajectories, and computing runtime risk metrics such as Time-to-Collision
    (TTC)~\cite{cricitality_metrics}.
    \item \textbf{Clustering:} Aggregation of dynamic points into coherent
  object-level clusters is required to enable object-centric reasoning and
  risk analysis. This abstraction also enables comparison with established
  perception architectures such as Autoware, which operate on object-level
  representations~\cite{autowarefoundationPerceptionComponent}.
  \item \textbf{Criticality estimation:} The system must support online
computation of risk metrics that quantify the potential for collision or unsafe
interactions based on relative object motion and the planned ego trajectory.
This includes metrics such as Time-to-Collision (TTC), which are widely used in
automated driving to assess dynamic risk levels~\cite{cricitality_metrics}.
  \item \textbf{Modular ROS2 architecture:} Each processing stage is encapsulated
as an independent ROS2 package to enable isolated development, testing, and
maintenance. This modular structure facilitates the improvement of individual
components without affecting the rest of the system and supports the
interchangeability of alternative implementations for tasks such as depth
estimation, clustering, or criticality evaluation.

  \item \textbf{Robustness to occlusion:} Reliable operation in the presence of
partial or intermittent visibility is essential for real-world deployment.
Occlusion handling enhances the detection and tracking of dynamic objects even
when they are temporarily obscured (e.g. when two pedestrians cross
paths in opposite directions in front of the camera)

  \item \textbf{Portability and deployment:} The system must be packaged and
deployed using Docker to ensure compatibility with the software infrastructure
of the EDGAR research platform~\cite{edgar_hardware}. Containerization facilitates integration with
existing components, simplifies dependency management, and enables consistent
runtime behavior across development and deployment environments.

\end{enumerate}

These requirements are derived from both the theoretical analysis in the
literature and the practical constraints of the target deployment platform.

\section{System Architecture}\label{sec:SystemArchitecture}

This section introduces the high-level architecture of the system developed in this thesis.

The overall system is structured into two principal pipelines: the \textbf{perception pipeline} 
and the \textbf{criticality pipeline}, as introduced before. The perception pipeline operates on raw stereo image data, 
constructing a dynamic model of the environment by estimating the 3D positions and velocity vectors 
of moving objects. Its output is a structured list of detected objects, which serves as input to 
the criticality pipeline. The criticality pipeline evaluates the relative risk posed by each object with respect 
to the planned trajectory of the ego-vehicle, by computing criticality metrics.

\subsection{Perception pipeline}\label{sec:PerceptionPipeline}  

The perception pipeline will be responsible for detecting moving objects and estimating their position and current velocity (the 6D motion field). 
The pipeline consists of several submodules as illustrated in figure~\ref{fig:perception_pipeline_high_level}.

\begin{figure}[H]

As shown in the figure, the perception pipeline comprises the following core modules: 

\begin{enumerate}

Each of the previous presented modules will be implemented as a separate ROS2 package, allowing for

\section{Perception pipeline: Stereo image computation}\label{sec:PerceptionPipelineStereoImageComputation}

The stereo image computation module is responsible for generating a dense depth map from a pair of
rectified stereo images and its calibration parameters. This component is particularly relevant when 
working with datasets or sensors that provide raw stereo image pairs but no precomputed depth information. Although this
module is not used in the final deployment of the system on the EDGAR research vehicle, since its
stereo camera setup provides depth images directly, it can be used in the future to integrate other
stereo camera sets that do not provide depth images.

\subsection{Mathematical Background: Pinhole Camera Model and Disparity}

In stereo vision, depth estimation relies on the triangulation of corresponding points in a pair of 
images captured from slightly different viewpoints. The pinhole camera model (figure:~\ref{fig:pinhole_camera_model}) is commonly used to describe the 
relationship between 3D world coordinates and 2D image coordinates. In this model, a 3D point $\mathbf{P} = (X, Y, Z)$ 
is projected onto the image plane as a 2D point $\mathbf{p} = (x, y)$ using the camera's intrinsic parameters (\cite{pin_hole_camera_model}).
These parameters include the focal length $f$ and the principal point $(c_x, c_y)$, which define the optical center and field of view of the camara.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \resizebox{0.4\textwidth}{!}{\includegraphics{figures/pinhole_camera.png}}
  \caption{Pinhole camera model. Source: Nvidia~\label{fig:pinhole_camera_model}}
\end{figure}%

The relationship between the 3D point and its 2D projection is given by:

\begin{equation}
  \begin{bmatrix}
  x \\
  y \\
  1
  \end{bmatrix}
  = 
  \frac{1}{Z}
  \begin{bmatrix}
  f_x & 0 & c_x \\
  0 & f_y & c_y \\
  0 & 0 & 1
  \end{bmatrix}
  \begin{bmatrix}
  X \\
  Y \\
  Z
  \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

where $f_x$ and $f_y$ are the focal lengths in pixels, and $(c_x, c_y)$ is the principal point.

In a typical stereo setup, the two cameras are displaced horizontally along the x-axis, with their 
optical axes kept parallel. This physical separation between the cameras is known as the 
\textbf{baseline} $B$, as seen in the figure~\ref{fig:stereo_camera_setup_disparity} left. The baseline is a key parameter in stereo vision, as it directly influences 
the triangulation accuracy: a larger baseline improves depth resolution, especially for distant 
objects, but also increases the difficulty of finding correct correspondences due to greater viewpoint differences.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \begin{minipage}{0.343\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/framos_stereo_setup.jpg}
  \end{minipage}
  \hfill
  \begin{minipage}{0.62\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/disparity_map.png}
  \end{minipage}
 \caption{Stereo camera setup (left) and disparity concept (right). Source: Framos,~\cite{disp_map_figure}.}\label{fig:stereo_camera_setup_disparity}
\end{figure}


Thanks to this horizontal arrangement, the correspondence search simplifies to a one-dimensional 
problem along the image rows. After rectification, corresponding points between the two images lie 
along the same horizontal line (epipolar line), and the \textbf{disparity} $d$ (figure~\ref{fig:stereo_camera_setup_disparity} right) between corresponding 
points is defined as:

\begin{equation}
  d = x_{\text{left}} - x_{\text{right}}
\end{equation}
  
Disparity is inversely proportional to the depth of the observed point. The relation between disparity 
and depth $Z$ is given by:
  
\begin{equation}
Z = \frac{f \cdot B}{d}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Stereo Matching Algorithms}

Stereo matching algorithms are responsible for computing the disparity map between a pair of 
rectified stereo images. Given that disparity directly relates to depth, these algorithms are a 
critical component of the stereo vision pipeline. Their main task is to find, for each pixel in the 
left image, the corresponding pixel in the right image along the same horizontal line.

Several approaches exist to solve this correspondence problem, each with different trade-offs 
between computational complexity, robustness, and accuracy. Some of the most common implemented stereo
matching algorithms include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\gls{BM}:} A local method that compares fixed-size blocks between the left and right images. While computationally efficient, it often struggles with textureless regions and depth discontinuities (\cite{disparity_block_matching}). 
  \item \textbf{\gls{SGM}:} An algorithm that balances accuracy and computational efficiency by aggregating matching costs along multiple paths through the image (\cite{disparity_sgm}).
\end{itemize}

Comparative studies (\cite{disparity_survey}) have shown that SGM offers a good trade-off between accuracy and performance, making it suitable for real-time applications where both speed and quality are essential.


\subsection{Semi-Global Matching (SGM)}\label{sec:SGM}

\gls{SGM} is a stereo matching algorithm that offers a compromise between the 
accuracy of global optimization methods and the computational efficiency of local methods. It was first
introduced by Hirschmüller~\cite{disparity_sgm} and has become widely implemented in real-time 
applications.

The underlying principle in SGM is to minimize a global energy function that imposes continuity in the
disparity map, having strong depth discontinuities along object boundaries. The energy function $E(D)$
for a disparity map $D$ is defined as:

\begin{equation}
E(D) = \sum_{\mathbf{p}} \left( C(\mathbf{p}, D_{\mathbf{p}}) + \sum_{\mathbf{q} \in \mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{p}}} 
P_1 \cdot \delta\left( |D_{\mathbf{p}} - D_{\mathbf{q}}| = 1 \right) + P_2 \cdot \delta\left( |D_{\mathbf{p}} - D_{\mathbf{q}}| > 1 \right) \right)
\end{equation}

where:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $C(\mathbf{p}, D_{\mathbf{p}})$ is the matching cost at pixel $\mathbf{p}$ for disparity $D_{\mathbf{p}}$.
  \item $\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{p}}$ denotes the set of neighboring pixels.
  \item $P_1$ and $P_2$ are penalty terms for disparity changes of 1 and greater than 1, respectively.
  \item $\delta$ is the Kronecker delta function.
\end{itemize}

The algorithm operates in two main steps:

\textbf{1. Cost computation:}  
For each possible disparity, a matching cost calculation happens between the left picture plus 
the right picture at each pixel.

\textbf{2. Cost aggregation:}  
SGM does not do a full 2D global optimization. That process demands a lot of computation, so it 
is not practical for systems that need to run in real time. SGM comes close to global reasoning when 
it combines costs along several 1D paths. Costs are put together along either 4 or 8 directions from
each pixel. A dynamic programming method finds the lowest local cost in each direction. 
It also adds penalties for significant changes in disparity.

Once the cost values are added together from all directions, the disparity picked for each pixel is the one that has the lowest combined cost.


This balance between accuracy and computational efficiency makes SGM highly attractive for embedded 
and real-time applications. 

\subsection{ROS2 Implementation}\label{sec:ROS2Implementation}

The SGM algorithm has been integrated into the pipeline with a dedicated ROS2 package called 
\texttt{stereo\_computation}.It contains a node that subscribes to three topics: the rectified left and right 
camera images, and the camera info topic of the left camera. 
After receiving synchronized input, the node computes both the disparity map and the depth map, 
which are then published on two separate ROS2 topics for downstream processing.

The stereo matching algorithm was implemented using the OpenCV library. In particular, the 
\texttt{cv::cuda::StereoSGM} class was chosen to compute the disparity map. This class offers a 
CUDA-accelerated version of the Semi-Global Matching algorithm, enabling the computations to be 
performed directly on the GPU. Taking advantage of GPU acceleration is crucial to meet the real-time 
performance requirements of the perception pipeline.

The OpenCV implementation of SGM is highly optimized and designed to exploit the parallel 
architecture of modern GPUs, significantly reducing the runtime compared to \gls{CPU} basedmethods. 
After computing the disparity map, the depth map is generated by applying the classical 
pinhole camera equation:

\begin{equation}
Z = \frac{f \cdot B}{d}
\end{equation}

where $Z$ is the depth value, $f$ is the focal length of the camera, $B$ is the baseline distance 
between the two cameras, and $d$ is the disparity.

This node architecture ensures modularity within the perception pipeline and allows easy 
integration with other modules, such as the Kalman filtering and object detection components. 
Furthermore, by publishing both the disparity and depth images, the system maintains flexibility 
for future extensions or algorithm improvements that may require direct access to raw disparity data.

\section{Perception pipeline: Optical flow computation}\label{sec:PerceptionPipelineOpticalFlowComputation}

This section describes the optical flow computation module within the perception pipeline.
This module is responsible for estimating the relative motion of the pixels of the image between
two consecutive frames.

\subsection{Optical flow concept}\label{sec:OpticalFlowConcept}
The concept of Optical Flow, first introduced by James Gibson in the 1950s (\cite{optical_flow_concept}) 
refers to the displacements of intensity patterns.  In our case it represents the relative motion 
of the pixels between two consequtive frames of a video stream. The output is  a 2D vector field where 
each vector describes the position of a pixel or a small region in the current frame relative to its 
position in the previous frame.

This module is crucial for estimating the information of how the pixels in the image are moving between frames, which is
one of the essential inputs to the Kalman Filter stage. 

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \resizebox{0.6\textwidth}{!}{\includegraphics{figures/opt_flw_concept.png}}
  \caption{Optical flow concept}\label{fig:optical_flow_concept}
\end{figure}%

Optical flow estimation relies on the assumotion that the brightness of a pixel remains constant
over time \cite{optical_flow_review_2025,optical_flow_computation_survey}, which allows the system to track the movement of objects based on the changes in pixel
intensity.  Mathematically, this assumption leads to the optical flow constraint equation:
\begin{equation}
  I_x u + I_y v + I_t = 0
\end{equation}  

Where: 

\begin{itemize}
  \item $I_x$ and $I_y$ are the spatial gradients of the image intensity in the x and y directions.
  \item $I_t$ is the temporal gradient of the image intensity (the difference between two consecutive frames).
  \item $u$ and $v$ are the optical flow components in the x and y directions.
\end{itemize}

Since there is one equation and two unknowns, the optical flow constraint equation is underdetermined.
To solve this problem, additional constraints or assumptions are needed, leading to different 
optical flow algorithms.

\subsection{Optical flow algorithms}\label{sec:OpticalFlowAlgorithms}

Optical flow methods can be classified into two main categories: sparse and dense optical flow.

\textbf{Sparse optical flow} methods focus on estimating motion vectors at a discrete set of 
key points in the image. These points are typically features such as corners or edges that 
can be tracked reliably across frames. In contrast, \textbf{dense optical flow} methods aim 
to estimate a motion vector for every pixel in the image, providing a complete 2D 
displacement field.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \resizebox{0.8\textwidth}{!}{\includegraphics{figures/sparse_vs_coarse_opt_flw.jpg}}
  \caption{Sparse vs dense optical flow. Source: \cite{figure_sparse_vs_dense_opf_flw}}\label{fig:sparse_vs_dense_optical_flow}
\end{figure}%

Recently, deep learning-based methods have gained popularity and demonstrated impressive
performance for optical flow estimation. Models such as FlowNet~\cite{opt_flw_flownet} and
RAFT~\cite{optical_flw_raft} leverage convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to learn spatial and temporal
features from large datasets, enabling them to predict optical flow fields with high accuracy. However,
these methods often require significant computational resources and may suffer from poor
generalization to unseen environments.

For these reasons, and due to the necessity of robustness and real-time performance, this work focuses
on traditional deterministic optical flow algorithms. These classical methods also offer the advantage
of being widely implemented and optimized in existing libraries, which significantly accelerates
development and integration time.

Some of the most common optical flow algorithms include \cite{optical_flow_estimation_survey,opt_flw_old_survey}: 

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Lucas-Kanade method~\cite{flow_lucas_kanade}:}
  
  Introduces in 1981 by Lucas and Kanade, this os a sparse optical flow method based on the assumption
  of constant optical flow within a small local neighborhood. It solves the optical flow constraint
  equation using a least-squares approach. This method is simple and computationally efficient, but it is 
  sensitive to large motions and requires good texture in the tracked regions.

  \item \textbf{Horn-Schunck method~\cite{opt_flw_schunck}:}
  
  Proposed by Horn and Schunck, this method computed dense optical flow by introducing a global smoothness
  constraint. It formulates flow estimation as the minimization of an enegy function that combines the 
  brightness constancy constraint and a smoothness term. While robust to moredate noise and capable of estimating
  full dense flow, the method can oversmooth motion boundaries and struggles with large displacements.

  \item \textbf{TV-L1 method~\cite{opt_flw_tv_l1}:} 
  
  This approach refines the variational method of Horn and Schunck by introducing a total variation (TV) 
  regularization term and an L1 norm for the data term. The resulting algorithm is more robust and was 
  implemented in the previous work of Bauer \cite{previous_tfm_bauer}. Nevertheless is computationally
  expensive and may not be the best choice for real-time applications.

  \item \textbf{Farneback method~\cite{opf_flw_farneback}:}
  Farneback introduced a dense optical flow method based on local polynomial expansions. The idea is to 
  approximate the neighborhood of each pixel ith a quadratic polynomial and then estimate how the polynomials
  change between frames. 

  This method offers a good trade-off between accuracy and computational efficiency. When accelerated using
  GPUs (e.g. via OpenCV CUDA modules), it can achieve real-time performance even on high-resolution images.
\end{itemize}

In this work, the Farneback method has been selected due to its computational efficiency, robustness for medium-range motions,
and the availability of an optimized CUDA implementation.

\subsection{Farneback algorithm}
\label{sec:FarnebackAlgorithm}

The Farneback optical flow method is a dense flow estimation algorithm based on polynomial
expansion of local image neighborhoods. Its core idea is to approximate the intensity variation in
a small image region using a quadratic polynomial, and then infer the motion between frames by
analyzing how these polynomials deform over time.

The first step is to approximate the image intensity function $I(x, y)$ in a local neighborhood
using a second-order Taylor expansion:
\begin{equation}
I(x, y) \approx x^T A x + b^T x + c
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $x = (x, y)^T$ represents the spatial coordinates,
  \item $A$ is a symmetric $2\times2$ matrix encoding the second-order spatial derivatives,
  \item $b$ is a $2\times1$ vector representing the first-order spatial derivatives,
  \item $c$ is a scalar term corresponding to the local intensity offset.
\end{itemize}

This local polynomial approximation can be efficiently computed over the entire image using
Gaussian-weighted least squares fitting, a process known as polynomial expansion.

When the scene undergoes a pure translation $d = (d_x, d_y)^T$ between two consecutive frames,
the transformed intensity function $I'(x)$ can be related to the original by:
\begin{equation}
I'(x) = I(x - d)
\end{equation}

Substituting the polynomial approximation yields:
\begin{equation}
I'(x) \approx (x - d)^T A (x - d) + b^T (x - d) + c
\end{equation}

Expanding the right-hand side leads to:
\begin{equation}
I'(x) \approx x^T A x + (b - 2Ad)^T x + (d^T A d - b^T d + c)
\end{equation}

Comparing terms, we observe that:
\begin{align}
A' &= A \\
  b' &= b - 2Ad \\
  c' &= d^T A d - b^T d + c
\end{align}
where $A'$ and $b'$ represent the new polynomial coefficients after displacement.

Thus, the displacement $d$ can be estimated by solving the linear system:
\begin{equation}
2A d = b - b'
\end{equation}
assuming that $A$ is non-singular and well-conditioned.

In practice, direct estimation is complicated by noise, local deformations, and the limited validity of
polynomial approximations. To address these issues, the following refinements are applied:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Estimate the displacement over a local window using Gaussian weighting to prioritize central pixels.
  \item Aggregate local estimates using weighted least squares to improve robustness against noise.
  \item Employ a multi-scale coarse-to-fine pyramid approach to capture both small and large displacements effectively.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Coarse-to-fine pyramid approach}

Large displacements between frames can cause local matching failures if only a single resolution
is used. To handle this, the Farneback method employs a coarse-to-fine multi-scale pyramid strategy
\cite{opt_flw_coarse_to_fine}.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \resizebox{0.8\textwidth}{!}{\includegraphics{figures/pyramid.png}}
  \caption{Coarse-to-fine pyramid approach. Source: Matlab}\label{fig:opf_flw_pyramid}
\end{figure}%

In the pyramid approach:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The original images are repeatedly downsampled by a scaling factor (typically 0.5), producing
  several lower-resolution versions.
  \item Optical flow is first estimated at the coarsest (smallest) scale, where large motions appear reduced
  and easier to estimate.
  \item The initial flow estimate is then propagated to the next finer scale, where it is refined using the
  higher-resolution image data.
  \item This process continues until the finest scale (the original resolution) is reached, producing a dense
  and detailed optical flow field.
\end{itemize}

The pyramid-based coarse-to-fine strategy is crucial for ensuring that large motions do not overwhelm the local
polynomial approximation, maintaining both the accuracy and robustness of the Farneback method.

This hierarchical refinement significantly enhances the capability of the algorithm to deal with large
inter-frame displacements, dynamic scenes, and varying object velocities.

\section{Perception pipeline: Ego motion estimation}\label{sec:PerceptionPipelineEgoMotionEstimation}

In the context of autonomous and automated vehicles, estimating the ego motion is crucial to
understand how the vehicle is moving in the environment. In the scope of this work, ego motion is
defined as the relative motion of the vehicle with respect to the environment. Specifically, the 3D 
position of the camera in one frame, relative to the previous frame.

This information is essential for the Kalman Filter step where the 6D field will be estimated, since the optical flow output 
is relative to the camera frame, and the Kalman Filter requires knowledge of the camera's position in the world frame
to properly compensate for its motion.

For this purpose, \gls{VO} techniques are used to estimate the required information
using the stereo setup input. Incorporating the depth information from the stereo camera allows the
direct recovery of metric information, providing robustness and accuracy.

This section describes the concept behind \gls{VO}, the coordinate systems used, the specific
algorithmic approach that was implemented, as well as the ROS2 implementation details.

\subsection{Visual odometry}
\label{sec:VisualOdometryConcept}

\gls{VO} refers to the process of estimating the motion of a camera by analyzing a
sequence of images it captures. Originally introduced by Moravec~\cite{vo_moravec} and later
formalized by Nist\'er et al.~\cite{vo_nister}, \gls{VO} has become a fundamental technique for mobile
robotics and autonomous driving~\cite{visual_odometry_review}.

The core idea behind \gls{VO} is to track visual features across consecutive frames and infer the relative
motion of the camera from these observations. The estimated motion provides a continuous estimate
of the vehicle trajectory without relying on external localization systems.

\gls{VO} methods are generally categorized based on the type of input and processing
approach used:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Monocular \gls{VO}:} Utilizes a single camera as input. It is cost-effective but suffers from
  scale ambiguity, meaning that the absolute scale of the motion (e.g., in meters) cannot be determined
  without additional information. Monocular \gls{VO} is often used in conjunction with other sensors
  (e.g., \gls{IMU}) to resolve this ambiguity~\cite{visual_odometry_review}.

  \item \textbf{Stereo \gls{VO}:} Used two synchronized cameras to estimate depth through triangulation,
  which allows direct recovery of metric scale. This removes the scale ambiguity present in monocular
  systems, producing more accurate and robust motion estimation. Stereo \gls{VO} is especially beneficial
  in environments where monocular methods struggle, such as in low-texture areas or during large
  baseline movements, thanks to the additional geometric constraints provided by the known baseline
  distance between the cameras~\cite{visual_odometry_review}.

\end{itemize}

Processing approaches are typically divided into two categories:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Feature-based methods:} Extract and match features (e.g., corners, edges) across frames,
  estimating motion from the displacement of these features.

  \item \textbf{Direct methods:} Directly use pixel intensities to estimate motion without explicit
  feature extraction.
\end{itemize}

Recently, \textbf{deep learning-based visual odometry} approaches have emerged, combining
different types neural networks to estimate motion directly from image sequences.
These methods aim to improve robustness under challenging conditions, such as illumination
changes and textureless environments~\cite{visual_odometry_review}.

This work adopts a \textbf{Stereo \gls{VO}} and \textbf{stereo feature-based} since the depth information is already available
from the stereo Camera and using feature matching to estimate motion robustly. This choice provides a good
balance between accuracy and computational efficiency, making it suitable for real-time applications

\subsection{Coordinate systems}\label{sec:ReferenceFramesCoordinateSystems and ROS2 transforms}

To properly understand the visual odometry algorithm and this work, it is important to define the
coordinate systems used in the implementation. The following coordinate frames are
defined in the context of this project:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{camera\_optical\_frame} The optical coordinate frame attached to the camera at the current time step. All of the optical frames follow
the ROS2 convention, where the z-axis points forward, the x-axis points to the right of the camera, and the y-axis points downwards.

\item \textbf{camera\_optical\_frame\_previous} The optical coordinate frame attached to the camera at the previous time step of
the visual odometry computation.

\item \textbf{camera\_optical\_frame\_initial} The coordinate frame attached to the camera at the start of the sequence.
\item \textbf{world} Coincident with camera camera\_optical\_frame\_initial but rotated. Z-axis points up, x-axis points forward, and y-axis points to the left.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Visual odometry algorithm}\label{sec:OverviewAlgorithm}

The visual odometry algorithm implemented in this work follows a stereo vo and feature-based stereo approach. It
estimates the relative motion of the camera by detecting and matching visual features between
consecutive frames, reconstructing their 3D positions using the depth image, and solving the camera
pose using a \gls{PnP} algorithm~\cite{vo_pnp_survey} with \gls{RANSAC} for outlier rejection.

A schematic overview of the algorithm steps is presented in Algorithm~\ref{alg:visual_odometry}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Stereo Visual Odometry Pipeline}\label{alg:visual_odometry}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \Require RGB images $I_{k-1}$, $I_k$, and depth image $D_{k-1}$
  \Ensure Estimated relative pose $(R, t)$ between frames
  \State Detect ORB features in $I_{k-1}$ and $I_k$ using GPU acceleration
  \State Match ORB features between $I_{k-1}$ and $I_k$
  \State Reproject matched keypoints from $I_{k-1}$ into 3D using $D_{k-1}$
  \State Solve \gls{PnP} with RANSAC using 3D-2D correspondences
  \State Refine the pose estimation with standard solve\gls{PnP} using inliers
  \State Estimate covariance based on reprojection residuals
  \State Apply statistical filtering and optional exponential smoothing
  \State Output $(R, t)$, covariance matrix, and debug images
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The code of the algorithm is implemented without any ROS2 dependencies, allowing it to be
easily reused in other projects. In the next sections, the main steps of the algorithm are
described in detail.
  
\subsubsection{Feature detection and matching}\label{sec:FeatureDetectionMatching}

Feature detection and matching constitute the first step in the visual odometry pipeline. In this phase, 
some points, that will serve to compute the desired transformation, will be detected in two consequtive frames.
For this, the \gls{ORB}~\cite{vo_orb} algorithm will be used.

\gls{ORB} is a binary feature descriptor that builds upon two classical methods: \gls{FAST}~\cite{vo_fast} for keypoint detection and \gls{BRIEF}~\cite{vo_brief} for descriptor computation. 

In this context, a keypoint is a point in the image that is distinctive and repeatable (typically corners or blobs), that can be reliably identified across multiple frames.
Around each keypoint, a descriptor is computed: a compact representation of the visual appearance in its local neighborhood. 
These descriptors allow the system to match corresponding features between frames, even under variations in viewpoint.

\gls{FAST} is a corner keypoint detection algorithm that quickly identifies points in the image with significant intensity
change compared to their surroundings. \gls{BRIEF}, on the other hand, generates a binary string descriptor
by comparing the intensity of random pairs of pixels in a small patch around the keypoint.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \resizebox{0.4\textwidth}{!}{\includegraphics{figures/FAST.jpg}}
  \caption{FAST algorithm. Source: \cite{IntroductionFAST}}
\end{figure}%

\gls{ORB} enhances these methods by introducing rotation and scale invariance. The orientation of each
keypoint is computed to make \gls{BRIEF} descriptors rotation-invariant, and a multi-scale pyramid is used
to handle changes in scale. 

The feature extraction and matching process is performed on the GPU using the CUDA modules of OpenCV.
This allows real-time processing even at high image resolutions.

The steps involved are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Detect \gls{ORB} features separately in the previous and current grayscale images.
  \item Compute binary descriptors for each detected feature.
  \item Match descriptors between the previous and current frames using brute-force Hamming distance.
  \item Extract the coordinates of matched keypoints for subsequent motion estimation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{3D-2D correspondences}\label{sec:3D2DCorrespondences}

Once the feature matching step is completed, the next task is to establish 3D-2D correspondences
between the previous and current frames. This is necessary for estimating the camera motion through
\gls{PnP} techniques.

For each matched keypoint in the previous frame, the corresponding depth value is retrieved from the
depth image associated with that frame. If the depth value is within a valid range, the 2D keypoint is
reprojected into a 3D point using the intrinsic parameters of the stereo camera.

The reprojection follows the standard pinhole camera model:

\begin{equation}
X = (u - c_x) \cdot \frac{d}{f_x}, \quad
Y = (v - c_y) \cdot \frac{d}{f_y}, \quad
Z = d
\end{equation}

where $(u, v)$ are the pixel coordinates of the keypoint, $(c_x, c_y)$ are the coordinates of the principal
point, $(f_x, f_y)$ are the focal lengths in pixels, and $d$ is the depth value.

This process results in a set of valid 3D points extracted from the previous frame, and their corresponding
2D projections detected in the current frame. These 3D-2D pairs are later used to estimate the relative
pose of the camera.

If the depth value is invalid (e.g., missing or outside the valid range), the corresponding match is
discarded to avoid introducing noise into the motion estimation process. In the followign figure, a 
set of detected keypoint can be seen. The ones with valid depth are shown in green, while the ones with invalid depth are shown in red.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \resizebox{1.0\textwidth}{!}{\includegraphics{figures/Keypoints.png}}
  \caption{Odometry Keypoints with depth filtering and RGB Image}\label{fig:3D2D_correspondences}
\end{figure}%

\subsubsection{Motion estimation using PnP and RANSAC}\label{sec:MotionEstimationPnP}

Once 3D-2D correspondences are available, the relative camera motion between two frames can be 
estimated by solving the \gls{PnP} problem~\cite{vo_pnp_survey}. The goal is to compute the rotation 
$\mathbf{R}$ and translation $\mathbf{t}$ that map the 3D points observed in the previous frame to 
their 2D projections in the current image.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \resizebox{0.4\textwidth}{!}{\includegraphics{figures/PnP.jpg}}
  \caption{Perspectective-n-Point (PnP) problem. Source: \cite{vo_pnp_opencv}}\label{fig:pnp}
\end{figure}%

Given a set of $n$ correspondences $\{ (\mathbf{X}_i, \mathbf{x}_i) \}$, where $\mathbf{X}_i$ are 3D 
points in the previous frame and $\mathbf{x}_i$ their 2D image projections in the current frame, 
\gls{PnP} finds a transformation that minimizes the reprojection error under the pinhole camera model:

\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}
u \\ v \\ 1
\end{bmatrix}
\sim
\mathbf{K}
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{R} & \mathbf{t}
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
X \\ Y \\ Z \\ 1
\end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

Here, $\mathbf{K}$ is the camera intrinsic matrix, and $\mathbf{R}, \mathbf{t}$ represent the relative 
pose of the current frame with respect to the previous one. The symbol $\sim$ denotes equality up to 
scale in homogeneous coordinates.

In practice, 3D-2D correspondences may include mismatches or noisy observations. To robustly estimate 
the pose in the presence of such outliers, \gls{RANSAC} is used in conjunction 
with \gls{PnP}.

\gls{RANSAC} operates by randomly selecting minimal subsets of correspondences, solving the \gls{PnP} problem for 
each subset, and evaluating how many correspondences agree with the estimated pose. The solution with 
the highest number of inliers is selected, and the pose is then refined using all inliers to minimize 
the overall reprojection error.

In this work, the function \texttt{cv::solvePnPRansac} from OpenCV is used to perform motion estimation. 
This function takes as input the 3D points from the previous frame, their 2D projections in the current 
frame, and the camera intrinsics, and returns the estimated rotation and translation vectors.

The steps involved are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Project 3D points from the previous frame into the current image using candidate pose.
  \item Measure the reprojection error against actual 2D keypoints.
  \item Select inliers whose error is below a given threshold.
  \item Repeat for several random subsets and select the best solution.
  \item Refine the pose using all inliers.
\end{enumerate}

The resulting output consists of:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{rvec} a rotation vector in axis-angle form.
  \item \texttt{tvec} a 3D translation vector.
\end{itemize}

The \texttt{rvec} returned by \texttt{solvePnPRansac} is a Rodrigues rotation vector or axis-angle rotation vector, 
a compact 3×1 representation of rotation. The direction of the vector indicates the axis of rotation, while its 
magnitude represents the rotation angle in radians around that axis. This vector can be converted to a rotation matrix
using the Rodrigues formula.

This matrix describes the transformation from the previous camera frame to the current one, and can 
be used for pose chaining, trajectory estimation, or visual odometry back-end integration.


\subsubsection{Covariance estimation}\label{sec:CovarianceEstimation}

After estimating the camera pose using \gls{PnP} and \gls{RANSAC}, it is often useful to quantify the uncertainty 
associated with this estimate. This is particularly relevant when integrating the pose into filtering 
frameworks, such as an \gls{EKF}, where a covariance is required.

In this work, the pose covariance is approximated based on the reprojection residuals of the inlier 
correspondences identified by \gls{RANSAC}. Once the best pose has been selected, all 3D points are projected 
onto the image using the estimated transform, and the residual reprojection errors are computed as:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{e}_i = \mathbf{x}_i^{\text{observed}} - \mathbf{x}_i^{\text{projected}}(R, t)
\end{equation}

The residuals are stacked and used to compute a sample covariance matrix:

\begin{equation}
\Sigma = \frac{1}{N-1} \sum_{i=1}^N (\mathbf{e}_i - \bar{\mathbf{e}})(\mathbf{e}_i - \bar{\mathbf{e}})^T
\end{equation}


Although this approach does not yield an exact pose covariance, it provides a reasonable and fast 
approximation that reflects the quality of the inlier set and the geometric configuration of the 
matched points.


\subsubsection{Motion filtering and smoothing}\label{sec:MotionFilteringSmoothing}

The pose estimates obtained via \gls{PnP} are subject to noise due to imperfect keypoint matching, depth
errors, or small instabilities in the feature geometry. To improve the consistency of the estimated
trajectory, a filtering and smoothing step is applied.
Two approaches are combined:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Statistical filtering:} To detect and remove outliers in both translation and rotation
  components based on deviation from a local history.
  \item \textbf{Exponential smoothing:} To smooth the motion estimate over time using a weighted moving
  average.
\end{itemize}

Every component of the translation and rotation vector is  separately. A sliding window is stored for each component to compute its mean and
standard deviation. New values that exceed a fixed threshold or deviate significantly from the mean 
are replaced with the mean.

Once filtered, the values are smoothed using exponential smoothing:

\begin{equation}
s_t = \alpha x_t + (1 - \alpha) s_{t-1}
\end{equation}

where $s_t$ is the smoothed value at time $t$, $x_t$ is the current value, and $\alpha$ is a smoothing
factor between 0 and 1.

Rotation vectors are smoothed component-wise in Rodrigues form. Although this is a simplification
compared to quaternion-based smoothing on $SO(3)$, it performs well in practice for small motions.

After filtering and smoothing, the pose is converted to a 4x4 transformation matrix and stored as the
final motion estimate for the current frame.

\subsection{ROS2 Implementation}

\section{Perception pipeline: Kalman filter}\label{sec:KalmanFilter}

After introducing the modules responsible for the depth computation (\ref{sec:PerceptionPipelineStereoImageComputation}), optical flow computation (\ref{sec:PerceptionPipelineOpticalFlowComputation}),
and ego-motion estimation (\ref{sec:PerceptionPipelineEgoMotionEstimation}), this section addresses the core component of the perception 
pipeline: the estimation of a dense six-dimensional field using a Kalman filter. 

The objective of this stage is to compute, for a subset of selected pixels of the image, both their 3D position and 3D
velocity over time. This results in a per-pixel six-dimensional vector that captures the spatial and
dynamic state of the scene.

This idea aligns with the concept of 6D vision (see Section~\ref{sec:6D_Vision}),
which was initially explored at the FTM Chair by Bauer~\cite{previous_tfm_bauer} and later validated in a
ROS implementation by Pastor~\cite{previous_tfm_pastor}.

Each point (tracked pixel) is represented by a WorldPoint object, which maintains its own Kalman 
filter and a 6D state vector composed of 3D position and velocity in world coordinates. 
At each timestep, the state of the point is updated using three main sources of input: 

\begin{itemize} 
  \item Optical flow, providing pixel-level motion information between frames. 
  \item Depth data, used to reconstruct the 3D location of the point via reprojection. 
  \item Ego-motion, which accounts for the movement of the camera relative to the environment. 
\end{itemize}


The Kalman filter follows a standard constant-velocity model in 3D space. Predicted states are projected back into 
the image frame for association with new measurements, producing real-time filtered estimates of position and velocity 
at pixel level resolution.

This approach differs from object-based or grid-based tracking by focusing on local, independent point estimation. 
Points are initialized or removed based on visibility, depth consistency, and motion reliability. 
A regular grid helps initialize new points, and the use of OpenMP~\cite{openMP} allows for paralellizing the updates of all active filters
tracked points, leading to a significant speedup in the computation.

The system architecture separates the Kalman logic into a core module that operates independently of ROS2, 
and a ROS2 node that manages communication, timing, and visualization. The output includes a 6D image representation, 
debug data, and visualization markers for use in tools such as RViz and Foxglove. 


\subsection{Kalman filter algorithm}\label{sec:KalmanFilterAlgorithm}

In this module, each selected pixel or \texttt{WorldPoint} is associated with its own Kalman Filter
instance that estimates their local state. The filters operate independently and are updated
at each timestep using new input from the previous perception modules. This approach allows the system 
to maintain a dense spatial resolution while exploit paralell processing capabilities. 

The Kalman Filter, first introduced by R.E. Kalman in 1960~\cite{kalman}, is a recursive 
estimation algorithm, designed for linear, discrete-time dynamical systems with Gaussian noise. 
It estimates the internal state of a system by predicting its evolution over time and correcting
the prediction using new measurements. Taking this into account, the first thing that is necessary 
to implement a Kalman filter is to have the called \textbf{system model} and the \textbf{measurement model}. 

The system model is a state space representation of the system, which describes how the state evolves
over time. It is defined by the following equations:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{x}_k = \mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{x}_{k-1} + \mathbf{B} \cdot \mathbf{u}_k + \boldsymbol{\omega}_k
\end{equation}

Here, $\mathbf{x}_k$ is the state vector at time $k$, $\mathbf{A}$ is the state transition matrix, 
$\mathbf{u}_k$ is the control input, $\mathbf{B}$ is the control input matrix, and $\boldsymbol{\omega}_k$ 
is the process noise, assumed to be zero-mean Gaussian with covariance $\mathbf{Q}$:

\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{\omega}_k \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{Q})
\end{equation}

The measurement model describes how the measurements are related to the state. It is defined by the
following equations:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{z}_k = \mathbf{H} \cdot \mathbf{x}_k + \boldsymbol{\nu}_k
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{z}_k$ is the measurement vector, $\mathbf{H}$ is the measurement matrix, and 
$\boldsymbol{\nu}_k$ is the measurement noise, assumed to be zero-mean Gaussian with covariance 
$\mathbf{R}$:

\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{\nu}_k \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{R})
\end{equation}

When both models are linear, as in the equations above, the standard Kalman filter can be used. However, if 
either model is nonlinear, the \gls{EKF}~\cite{ekf} must be applied. The \gls{EKF} approximates 
nonlinear functions by linearizing them around the current state estimate using the Jacobian (first order Taylor series approximation).

\subsubsection{Standard Kalman filter}\label{sec:KalmanFilterClassic}

The standard Kalman filter operates in two main phases at each time step: prediction and correction. Each
phase consists of a sequence of matrix operations that update both the estimated state and its associated
covariance based on the system and measurement models. 

\paragraph{Prediction step}

The prediction step starts by propagating the previous
filtered state $\mathbf{x}_{k-1}$ forward in time using the system model. This results in the predicted state:

\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathbf{x}}_k = \mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{x}_{k-1} + \mathbf{B} \cdot \mathbf{u}_k
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{A}$ is the state transition matrix, $\mathbf{B}$ the control input matrix, and 
$\mathbf{u}_k$ the control vector at time $k$. The predicted covariance $\hat{\mathbf{P}}_k$ is then computed as:

\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathbf{P}}_k = \mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{P}_{k-1} \cdot \mathbf{A}^\top + \mathbf{Q}
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{P}_{k-1}$ is the previous covariance and $\mathbf{Q}$ is the process noise covariance. 

\paragraph{Correction step}

Once the state and covariance have been predicted, the correction step begins and the expected measurement is computed using the measurement model:

\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathbf{z}}_k = \mathbf{H} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{x}}_k
\end{equation}

The predicted measurement covariance is then:

\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathbf{T}}_k = \mathbf{H}_k \cdot \hat{\mathbf{P}}_k \cdot \mathbf{H}_k^\top
\end{equation}

When a new measurement $\mathbf{z}_k$ is available, it is compared with the predicted measurement 
$\hat{\mathbf{z}}_k = \mathbf{h}(\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{w|k})$ to evaluate how consistent the prediction is 
with the actual observation. The difference between them is known as the innovation vector:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{s}_k = \mathbf{z}_k - \hat{\mathbf{z}}_k
\end{equation}

This vector represents the residual between what the filter expected to observe and what was actually measured. 
If the model and previous state are accurate, the innovation should be small and centered around zero. 
Large values in $\mathbf{s}_k$ may indicate unexpected changes in the system or incorrect predictions.

To properly weight the innovation in the update step, its uncertainty must be quantified. This is given by the 
innovation covariance matrix:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{S}_k = \hat{\mathbf{T}}_k + \mathbf{T}_k
\end{equation}

Here, $\hat{\mathbf{T}}_k$ is the predicted measurement covariance derived from the current state estimate, and 
$\mathbf{T}_k$ is the measurement noise covariance, defined by the sensor model. The matrix $\mathbf{S}_k$ thus 
quantifies the total expected uncertainty in the innovation vector, combining both predicted state uncertainty 
projected into measurement space and known sensor noise.

The innovation vector and its covariance are crucial for the correction step of the filter: they determine how 
strongly the measurement should influence the state update. A large $\mathbf{S}_k$ implies high uncertainty, 
which results in a smaller correction; conversely, a small $\mathbf{S}_k$ leads to a more aggressive update.

Once the innovation and its covariance are computed, the Kalman gain $\mathbf{K}_k$ is calculated. The Kalman gain
is a matrix that determines how much the predicted state should be adjusted based on the new measurement. It is computed as:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{K}_k = \hat{\mathbf{P}}_k \cdot \mathbf{H}^\top \cdot \mathbf{S}_k^{-1}
\end{equation}

Finally, the state and covariance are updated using the Kalman gain and the innovation vector. The updated state
is given by:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{x}_k = \hat{\mathbf{x}}_k + \mathbf{K}_k \cdot \mathbf{s}_k
\end{equation}

and the updated covariance becomes:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{P}_k = \left( \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{K}_k \cdot \mathbf{H} \right) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{P}}_k
\end{equation}

This completes one full iteration of the standard Kalman filter. The filtered state $\mathbf{x}_k$ and
covariance $\mathbf{P}_k$ serve as the input for the next prediction step.

\subsubsection{Extended Kalman filter}\label{sec:ExtendedKalmanFilter}

When the system or measurement model is nonlinear, the standard Kalman filter cannot be applied directly.
In such cases, the Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) is used. The \gls{EKF} extends the classical Kalman filter by
linearizing the nonlinear functions around the current estimate using a first-order Taylor expansion.

Let $\mathbf{f}(\cdot)$ represent the nonlinear system model, and $\mathbf{h}(\cdot)$ the nonlinear measurement
model. The \gls{EKF} performs the prediction step by applying the nonlinear dynamics to the previous state:

\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathbf{x}}_k = \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k-1}, \mathbf{u}_k)
\end{equation}

The predicted covariance is obtained using the Jacobian $\mathbf{F}_k$ of $\mathbf{f}(\cdot)$ with respect to
the state:

\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathbf{P}}_k = \mathbf{F}_k \cdot \mathbf{P}_{k-1} \cdot \mathbf{F}_k^\top + \mathbf{Q}
\end{equation}

The predicted measurement is computed by evaluating the nonlinear measurement function:

\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathbf{z}}_k = \mathbf{h}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_k)
\end{equation}

and its Jacobian with respect to the state is:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{H}_k = \left. \frac{\partial \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial \mathbf{x}} \right|_{\mathbf{x} = \hat{\mathbf{x}}_k}
\end{equation}

The predicted measurement covariance becomes:

\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathbf{T}}_k = \mathbf{H}_k \cdot \hat{\mathbf{P}}_k \cdot \mathbf{H}_k^\top
\end{equation}

Given a new measurement $\mathbf{z}_k$, the innovation is:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{s}_k = \mathbf{z}_k - \hat{\mathbf{z}}_k
\end{equation}

and the innovation covariance is:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{S}_k = \hat{\mathbf{T}}_k + \mathbf{R}
\end{equation}

Once the innovation and its covariance are computed, the Kalman gain is calculated as:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{K}_k = \hat{\mathbf{P}}_k \cdot \mathbf{H}_k^\top \cdot \mathbf{S}_k^{-1}
\end{equation}

Finally, the updated state and covariance are:

\begin{align}
  \mathbf{x}_k &= \hat{\mathbf{x}}_k + \mathbf{K}_k \cdot \mathbf{s}_k \\
  \mathbf{P}_k &= \left( \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{K}_k \cdot \mathbf{H}_k \right) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{P}}_k
\end{align}

This completes one full iteration of the Extended Kalman Filter. It retains the recursive structure of the
classical filter while enabling the integration of nonlinear system or measurement models by local
linearization.

\subsection{Mathematical model}\label{sec:KalmanFilterMathematicalModel}

Now that the Kalman filter algorithm has been introduced, we can define the mathematical model used
in this work. First, the system model is defined and then the measurement model will be defined. 

\subsubsection{System model}

For the system model, the state vector $\mathbf{x}_{k|w}$ is defined as a $6 \times 1$ vector that contains the
position and velocity of the tracked point in world coordinates ($w$):

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{x}_{k|w} =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    X \\
    Y \\
    Z \\
    \dot{X} \\
    \dot{Y} \\
    \dot{Z}
  \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

This vector is propagated over time using a constant-velocity motion model, assuming no acceleration between
frames. When no ego-motion compensation is applied, the evolution of the state is defined by the following 
linear state model system:

\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|w} = \mathbf{A}_{k|w} \cdot \mathbf{x}_{k-1|w} + \boldsymbol{\omega}_{k|w}
\end{equation}

where $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|w}$ is the predicted state at time $k$, $\mathbf{A}_{k|w}$ is the transition matrix 
that models constant velocity over the time step $\Delta t$, and $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k|w}$ is the process noise.

The state transition matrix $\mathbf{A}_{k|w}$ of the system is defined as: 

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{A}_{k|w} =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \mathbf{I}_3 & \Delta t \cdot \mathbf{I}_3 \\
    \mathbf{0}_3 & \mathbf{I}_3
  \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

where $\Delta t$ is the time elapsed between frames, representing the linear motion model. The process noise is assumed to be zero-mean Gaussian:

\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{\omega}_{k|w} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{Q}_{k|w})
\end{equation}

Where the process noise covariance matrix $\mathbf{Q}_{k|w}$ captures the uncertainty introduced during the 
state prediction. It is derived from the continuous-time system noise model~\cite{6d_vision_detailed} assuming white noise acceleration, 
and discretized using standard integration over the time interval \( \Delta t \). The resulting structure is:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{Q}_{k|w} =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \frac{1}{3} \Delta t^2 \cdot \text{diag}(\sigma^2_{\dot{X}}, \sigma^2_{\dot{Y}}, \sigma^2_{\dot{Z}}) &
    \frac{1}{2} \Delta t \cdot \text{diag}(\sigma^2_{\dot{X}}, \sigma^2_{\dot{Y}}, \sigma^2_{\dot{Z}}) \\
    \frac{1}{2} \Delta t \cdot \text{diag}(\sigma^2_{\dot{X}}, \sigma^2_{\dot{Y}}, \sigma^2_{\dot{Z}}) &
    \text{diag}(\sigma^2_{\dot{X}}, \sigma^2_{\dot{Y}}, \sigma^2_{\dot{Z}})
  \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}

This formulation assumes that velocity is affected by independent white noise processes in \( X \), \( Y \), and \( Z \), 
and that position integrates this uncertainty over time. As a result, the top-left block of \( \mathbf{Q}_{k|w} \)
corresponds to accumulated uncertainty in position, and the bottom-right to direct uncertainty in velocity.

To introduce the ego-motion compensation, we have to modify the system model. For this we assume, that the WorldPoints are 
transformed with the rotation matrix $\mathbf{R}_k$ and the translation vector $\mathbf{t}_k$, that model the movement of the 
camera between two frames (\ref{sec:MotionEstimationPnP}). The position and velocity components of the state vector $x_k$ are transformed 
as follows:


\begin{equation}
  \begin{bmatrix}
    X \\
    Y \\
    Z \\
  \end{bmatrix}_k
  =
  \mathbf{R}_k
  \cdot
  \begin{bmatrix}
    X \\
    Y \\
    Z \\
  \end{bmatrix}_{k-1|w}
  +
  \begin{bmatrix}
    t_x \\
    t_y \\
    t_z \\
  \end{bmatrix}_k
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \dot{X} \\
    \dot{Y} \\
    \dot{Z} \\
  \end{bmatrix}_k
  =
  \mathbf{R}_k
  \cdot
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \dot{X} \\
    \dot{Y} \\
    \dot{Z} \\
  \end{bmatrix}_{k-1|w}
\end{equation}

which leads to the following updated system state model: 

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{x}_k = \mathbf{A}_k \cdot \mathbf{x}_{k-1} + \mathbf{u}_k + \boldsymbol{\omega}_k
\end{equation}

Where the individual components are defined as:

\begin{align}
  \mathbf{A}_k &= \mathbf{D}_k \cdot \mathbf{A}_{k|w}, \in \mathbb{R}^{6 \times 6} \\
  \mathbf{D}_k &=
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \mathbf{R}_k & \mathbf{0}_3 \\
    \mathbf{0}_3 & \mathbf{R}_k
  \end{bmatrix}
  \in \mathbb{R}^{6 \times 6} \\
  \mathbf{u}_k &=
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \mathbf{t}_k \\
    \mathbf{0}_3
  \end{bmatrix}
  \in \mathbb{R}^{6 \times 1} \\
  \boldsymbol{\omega}_k &\sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{Q}_k)
\end{align}


Now that the ego-motion was introduced in the system model, the covariace matrix $\mathbf{Q}_k$ must be defined. 
This matrix is not only dependant on the process noise $\sigma^2_{\dot{X}}$, $\sigma^2_{\dot{Y}}$, and $\sigma^2_{\dot{Z}}$ like the covarianze matrix
without ego-motion $\mathbf{Q}_{k|w}$, but also on the ego-motion parameters. For this reason a parameter vector containing the ego-motion must be defined. 

In previous works like \cite{6d_vision_detailed}, \cite{previous_tfm_bauer}, and \cite{previous_tfm_pastor}, the ego-motion parameters were defined by the translation 
components and the Euler angles:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{p}_k =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    t_{k|x} & t_{k|y} & t_{k|z} & \theta_k & \phi_k & \psi_k
  \end{bmatrix}^T
\end{equation}

This approach has the big disadvantage that the Euler angles are not continuous and can lead to discontinuities in the
covariance matrix. Moreover, its necessary to know beforehand which Euler angle convention was used to build the rotation
matrix $\mathbf{R}_k$. This can lead to confusion and errors in the implementation and also limits the modularity of the code.

For this reason, in this work, the ego-motion rotation components are defined using the Rodrigues vector $\mathbf{r}_k$ that is computed from the rotation matrix $\mathbf{R}_k$:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{p}_k =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    t_{k|x} & t_{k|y} & t_{k|z} & r_{k|x} & r_{k|y} & r_{k|z}
  \end{bmatrix}^T
\end{equation}

The Euler-rodrigues vector, as introduced in the chapter \ref{sec:MotionEstimationPnP}, is a continuous representation of the rotation matrix as an vector in $\mathbb{R}^3$ where the length of the vector
is the angle of rotation and the direction of the vector is the axis of rotation \cite{euler_rodrigues_historical,euler_rodrigues} as seen in the figure \ref{fig:rodrigues}.
This representation is continuous and also enables the correct working of the algorithm regardless of how the rotation matrix was computed. 

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \resizebox{0.30\textwidth}{!}{\includegraphics{figures/euler_rodrigues_vector.png}}
  \caption{Rodrigues vector representation of the rotation matrix. Source:~\cite{euler_rodrigues_historical}}\label{fig:rodrigues}
\end{figure}

Now that the $p_k$ vector is defined, we can state that the rotation matrix $\mathbf{R}_k$ and the translation vector $\mathbf{t}_k$ are build as follows:

\begin{align}
  \mathbf{R}_k = \mathbf{R}(\mathbf{p}_k + \boldsymbol{\zeta}_k) \\
  \mathbf{t}_k = \mathbf{t}(\mathbf{p}_k + \boldsymbol{\zeta}_k)
\end{align}

Where $\boldsymbol{\zeta}_k$ is the additive white noise of $p_k$ and follows a Gaussian distribution with zero mean and covariance $\mathbf{L}$:

\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{\zeta}_k \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{L}), \quad \mathbf{L} = \text{diag}(\sigma^2_{t|x}, \sigma^2_{t|y}, \sigma^2_{t|z}, \sigma^2_{r|x}, \sigma^2_{r|y}, \sigma^2_{r|z}) \in \mathbb{R}^{6 \times 6}
\end{equation}

Taking this into account, the covariance matrix $\mathbf{Q}_k$ is defined as:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{Q}_k = \mathbf{D}_k \cdot \mathbf{Q}_{k|w} \cdot \mathbf{D}_k^\top + \mathbf{J}_k \cdot \mathbf{L} \cdot \mathbf{J}_k^\top
\end{equation}

In the previous equation, the term $J_k$ is the Jacobian of the state vector with respect to the ego-motion parameter vector $p_k$:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{J}_k = \left. \frac{\partial \mathbf{x}}{\partial \mathbf{p}} \right|_{(\mathbf{x}_{k-1}, \mathbf{p}_k)} \in \mathbb{R}^{6 \times 6}
\end{equation}
  
This Jacobian can be extended as follows:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{J}_k = \left. \frac{\partial \mathbf{x}}{\partial \mathbf{p}} \right|_{(\mathbf{x}_{k-1}, \mathbf{p}_k)} =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \left. \frac{\partial \mathbf{x}}{\partial \mathbf{t}_k} \right|_{(\mathbf{x}_{k-1}, \mathbf{p}_k)} & \left. \frac{\partial \mathbf{x}}{\partial \mathbf{r}_k}  \right|_{(\mathbf{x}_{k-1}, \mathbf{p}_k)} 
  \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}


The first term, the partial derivative with respect to $\mathbf{t}_k$, corresponds to the direct effect of 
translation on the predicted state. Since the translation vector only affects the position and not the 
velocity, the derivative is:

\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial \mathbf{x}_{k}}{\partial \mathbf{t}_k} =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \mathbf{I}_3 \\
    \mathbf{0}_3
  \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

The second term, the derivative with respect to the rotation vector $\mathbf{r}_k$, is more complex.
The rotation matrix $\mathbf{R}_k$ is computed from $\mathbf{r}_k$ using the Euler-Rodrigues formula.
In addition to the matrix itself, OpenCV provides the Jacobian 
$\frac{\partial \mathbf{R}_k}{\partial \mathbf{r}_k} \in \mathbb{R}^{9 \times 3}$, which encodes the derivative
of each element of $\mathbf{R}_k$ with respect to the rotation parameters.

Let us denote the intermediate propagated state before ego-motion as:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{p}_k = \mathbf{A}_{k|w} \cdot \mathbf{x}_{k-1|w} =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \mathbf{p}^{\text{pos}}_k \\
    \mathbf{p}^{\text{vel}}_k
  \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{p}^{\text{pos}}_k$ and $\mathbf{p}^{\text{vel}}_k$ are the position and velocity parts of the
propagated state. Then, the rotated position is given by 
$\mathbf{R}_k \cdot \mathbf{p}^{\text{pos}}_k$, and its derivative with respect to $\mathbf{r}_k$ is:

\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial (\mathbf{R}_k \cdot \mathbf{p}^{\text{pos}}_k)}{\partial \mathbf{r}_k} = 
  \sum_{i=1}^3 \left( \frac{\partial \mathbf{R}_k}{\partial r_i} \cdot \mathbf{p}^{\text{pos}}_k \right)
\end{equation}

The same procedure applies to the velocity component. The full Jacobian is then:

\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial \mathbf{x}_{k}}{\partial \mathbf{r}_k} =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \frac{\partial (\mathbf{R}_k \cdot \mathbf{p}^{\text{pos}}_k)}{\partial \mathbf{r}_k} \\
    \frac{\partial (\mathbf{R}_k \cdot \mathbf{p}^{\text{vel}}_k)}{\partial \mathbf{r}_k}
  \end{bmatrix}
  \in \mathbb{R}^{6 \times 3}
\end{equation}

Combining both components, the full Jacobian with respect to the motion parameters is:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{J}_{k|w} =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \mathbf{I}_3 & \frac{\partial (\mathbf{R}_k \cdot \mathbf{p}^{\text{pos}}_k)}{\partial \mathbf{r}_k} \\
    \mathbf{0}_3 & \frac{\partial (\mathbf{R}_k \cdot \mathbf{p}^{\text{vel}}_k)}{\partial \mathbf{r}_k}
  \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}


This expression captures how the uncertainty in the ego-motion parameters propagates into the predicted state 
covariance, and allows the filter to adapt to the quality of the visual odometry estimates in a principled way. 
The Jacobian \( \mathbf{J}_k \) is re-evaluated at each time step based on the current predicted state and ego-motion estimate.

Now that the full state prediction model, including ego-motion and its uncertainty, has been defined, we turn our attention 
to the measurement model, which relates the predicted state to the observations available from the stereo camera and 
optical flow pipeline.


\subsubsection{Measurement model}
\label{sec:KalmanFilterMeasurementModel}

The measurement model relates the predicted state to the observed measurements. In this case, the measurements are
obtained from the stereo camera and the optical flow computation. The measurements are defined as a $3 \times 1$ vector:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{z}_k =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    u_k \\
    v_k \\
    d_k
  \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
where \( u_k \) and \( v_k \) are the pixel coordinates of the tracked point in the image, and \( d_k \) is the depth.
In this project, the depth was considered for the measurment model, instead of the disparity like the previous works,
since most of the modern stereo cameras provide the depth directly as output. 

Unlike the system model, the measurement model is nonlinear. The relationship between the predicted state and the measurements is given by the 
pinhole camera model, which maps the 3D world coordinates to 2D pixel coordinates. The measurement model is defined as:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{z}_k = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{I}_3 & \mathbf{0}_3 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \frac{1}{w} \cdot \boldsymbol{\Pi} \cdot 
  \begin{pmatrix}
  X \\
  Y \\
  Z \\
  1
  \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}

where $\boldsymbol{\Pi}$ is the intrinsic projection matrix of the camera, defined as:

\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{\Pi} =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    f_x & 0 & c_x & 0 \\
    0 & f_y & c_y & 0 \\
    0 & 0 & 1 & 0
  \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

This projection transforms a 3D point in camera coordinates into homogeneous pixel coordinates. The final measured 
pixel location is obtained by normalizing the first two components by the third (perspective division), and taking 
the depth \( Z \) directly from the position vector. Therefore, the nonlinear measurement function 
$\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x}_{k})$ is defined as:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x}_{k}) =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \dfrac{f_x X}{Z} + c_x \\
    \dfrac{f_y Y}{Z} + c_y \\
    Z
  \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

This maps the predicted 3D point to the expected image coordinates $(u, v)$ and depth \( d = Z \). Since this model 
is nonlinear, the Extended Kalman Filter is used. To apply the correction step of the \gls{EKF}, the Jacobian of the 
measurement model with respect to the state vector is required.

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{H}_k = \left. \frac{\partial \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial \mathbf{x}} \right|_{\mathbf{x} = \hat{\mathbf{x}}_k}
\end{equation}

Only the position part of the state vector affects the measurement, so the Jacobian matrix $\mathbf{H}_k$ has 
non-zero entries only in the first three columns. The matrix $\mathbf{H}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 6}$ is computed as:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{H}_k =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \dfrac{f_x}{Z} & 0 & -\dfrac{f_x X}{Z^2} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
    0 & \dfrac{f_y}{Z} & -\dfrac{f_y Y}{Z^2} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
    0 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0
  \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

This Jacobian is evaluated at the predicted state $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k}$ and used in the computation of the innovation 
covariance.

\subsection{Implementation}\label{sec:ImplementationKalmanFilter}

Regarding the implementation of the filter, that will later be used by the corresponding
\texttt{KalmanFilterNode} ROS2 node, it is separated into two main classes as introduces before:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{WorldPoint}: This class represents a single tracked point in the scene and manages its own Kalman filter.
  \item \texttt{KalmanCore}: This class manages the list of all active filters and handles their initialization and updates.
\end{itemize}

In this subsection, we will describe the high level software architecture of both of them.

\subsubsection{WorldPoint class}\label{sec:WorldPointClassKalmanFilter}

The \texttt{WorldPoint} class is the core of the Kalman filter implementation. It encapsulates all the logic
for managing a single tracked point in the scene, including its Kalman filter, state prediction,
measurement update, and error code handling. In the following figure \ref{fig:wp} the class diagram of the \texttt{WorldPoint} class is shown.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[scale = 0.5]{figures/WorldPoint.png}
  \caption{WorldPoint class} %
  \label{fig:wp}
\end{figure}%

The initialization takes place through the method \texttt{initKalmanFilter()}, which takes a 
pixel location and a depth value, reprojects it to 3D world coordinates, and initializes the 
state vector and covariance. A regular grid ensures that only one point is initialized per 
cell, maintaining a uniform spatial distribution as will be explained in the following section.

The main function for updating each filter is \texttt{computeKalmanStep()}. It takes as input 
the depth image, optical flow, and the current ego-motion estimation. It begins by updating 
the measurement vector using optical flow, then retrieves the new depth from the depth image. 
If the new pixel lies outside the image boundaries or the depth is invalid, the function 
returns an error. Otherwise, it proceeds to the prediction step.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Kalman Filter Step (WorldPoint)}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \State Get optical flow at previous pixel position
  \State Compute new pixel and read new depth
  \If{new pixel is outside bounds or depth is invalid}
    \State \Return Error
  \EndIf
  \State Predict new state using motion model
  \If{using ego-motion}
    \State Apply rotation and translation
    \State Compute updated uncertainty using Jacobians
  \EndIf
  \State Project predicted state into image
  \State Compute innovation vector and covariance
  \If{3-sigma test fails}
    \State \Return Error
  \EndIf
  \State Compute Kalman gain
  \State Update state and covariance
  \State \Return Success
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

If ego-motion compensation is enabled, the predicted state is rotated and translated based on 
the estimated motion of the camera. Additionally, the associated uncertainty is propagated 
using Jacobians of the rotation matrix with respect to the Rodrigues vector. These Jacobians 
are computed using OpenCV, and allow precise propagation of uncertainty due to camera motion.

After predicting the state, the measurement is predicted by projecting the 3D point into the 
image using the pinhole camera model. This expected pixel and depth are then compared to the 
actual measurement, and an innovation vector is calculated. 

Before applying the correction step, a 3-sigma test is used to validate the measurement.
This test ensures that the observed measurement is statistically consistent with the
predicted measurement given the uncertainty. Specifically, it checks whether the Mahalanobis
distance of the innovation vector $\mathbf{s}_k$ is below a threshold:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{s}_k^\top \cdot \mathbf{S}_k^{-1} \cdot \mathbf{s}_k < \tau
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{S}_k$ is the innovation covariance and $\tau$ is the threshold corresponding
to a 99.7\% confidence interval in a chi-squared distribution with 3 s of freedom.
If the condition is not met, the measurement is considered an outlier and the correction step
is skipped to maintain filter robustness.


If the update is accepted, the Kalman gain is computed, and the state and covariance are 
updated accordingly. The filter also checks whether the updated 3D point lies within 
acceptable height bounds. If the point is too high or too low, it is discarded.



\subsubsection{KalmanCore class}\label{sec:KalmanCoreClass}

The \texttt{KalmanCore} class is responsible for managing the full set of active 
\texttt{WorldPoint} filters in the scene. It handles the initialization, update,
removal, and memory management of each tracked point, ensuring a scalable and spatially
uniform filtering process across the image.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[scale = 0.5]{figures/KalmanCore.png}
  \caption{KalmanCore class}

To guarantee an even distribution of tracked points, a grid-based approach is employed (figure \ref{fig:grid}).
The image plane is divided into a fixed number of rectangular cells, controlled by the
parameters \texttt{grid\_cols} and \texttt{grid\_rows}. Each cell holds at most one
active \texttt{WorldPoint}. This ensures that high-texture regions do not accumulate
redundant points, and low-texture areas are still covered, improving robustness across 
the entire field of view.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[scale = 0.3]{figures/gridApproach.png}
  \caption{KalmanCore grid}
  \label{fig:grid}
\end{figure}


The core logic of each frame is executed in the \texttt{updateSyncedData()} method. This
method is responsible for updating all active filters, removing invalid ones, and
reinitializing new points where necessary. The complete process is shown in 
Algorithm~\ref{alg:kalman_core_frame}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Frame Processing (\texttt{KalmanCore::updateSyncedData})}
  \label{alg:kalman_core_frame}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \State \textbf{Input:} depth image, optical flow, ego-motion estimate
    \State \textbf{Output:} updated set of WorldPoints
    \ForAll{grid cells $(i,j)$}
      \If{cell has an active WorldPoint}
        \State run \texttt{computeKalmanStep()} for the point
        \If{step fails or point outside bounds}
          \State remove WorldPoint from cell
        \EndIf
      \EndIf
    \EndFor
    \ForAll{empty cells $(i,j)$}
      \State search candidate pixels in image region
      \If{valid depth and point found}
        \State initialize new WorldPoint and store in grid
      \EndIf
    \EndFor
  \end{algorithmic}bb
\end{algorithm}

The algorithm is divided into two stages. In the first stage, each existing
\texttt{WorldPoint} is updated. If the prediction or correction fails, the point is
discarded. This may occur due to missing depth, invalid image location, or high
innovation error detected by the three-sigma test.

In the second stage, the algorithm checks for empty grid cells. For each unoccupied cell,
a new point is initialized by scanning its corresponding image region for valid pixels
with usable depth. The pixel is reprojected to 3D and passed to \texttt{initKalmanFilter()} 
to start a new filter instance.

The grid ensures that at least one point exists per region at any time, preventing
overlapping estimates and enabling scalable parallel execution. In addition,
the class supports multithreading: the outer loop is parallelized with
OpenMP, allowing all \texttt{WorldPoint}s to be updated simultaneously. This results in
a significant performance gain, especially when tracking hundreds of points across large 
resolutions.

\subsection{ROS2 integration}\label{sec:ROS2IntegrationKalmanFilter}

The \texttt{KalmanFilterNode} is the central ROS2 component that connects the Kalman 
filter logic with the perception pipeline. It handles all data flow by subscribing to 
synchronized data input streams, applying the filter update loop, and publishing processed 
outputs and debug information.

The node subscribes to three synchronized input topics: the depth image, the optical 
flow, and the ego-motion transform. Synchronization is performed using 
\texttt{message\_filters::Synchronizer} to guarantee time alignment across all data 
modalities. Accurate synchronization is crucial to prevent inconsistencies in the filter 
prediction and correction steps.

In addition, runtime behavior is fully configurable via ROS2 parameters. These include the grid 
size, filter parameters such height limits to consider, and whether ego-motion should be used. 
It also allows configuring all relevant noise sources via scalar parameters, 
including the standard deviations of optical flow, depth, process model, and ego-motion 
(rotational and translational) noise. These values are internally used to construct the 
covariance matrices required by the Kalman update equations.

The output of the node is a 2D image encoding the full 6D state field over the image

  \item Channel 6: binary validity flag (1.0 if valid, 0.0 if empty)
\end{itemize}

For qualitative evaluation and debugging, additional topics are published. 
These include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item A RGB image with the WorldPoint positions overlaid on the original image

\end{itemize}

\section{Perception pipeline: Object detection}\label{sec:PerceptionPipelineObjectDetection}

The final stage of the perception pipeline is the object detection component. It is
responsible for transforming the dense 6D motion field, produced by the Kalman filter,
into a compact set of object-level representations. Each detected object is described by
its spatial extent and estimated velocity, enabling further reasoning in downstream
modules such as criticality assessment.

The object detection component performs two main tasks. First, it filters and selects
reliable motion vectors from the raw state field. Then, it applies a clustering algorithm
to group consistent points into distinct objects. This approach allows the system to
transition from point-based to object-centric perception, which is better suited for
interpreting dynamic scenes.

Like the Kalman filter module, the object detection logic is implemented in a
ROS2-independent source library, and a ROS2 node handles communication and integration
with the rest of the system.

\subsection{Clustering}
\label{sec:Clustering}

The goal of the clustering stage is to aggregate motion vectors into object-level
groups. Each vector, defined by a 3D position and velocity, represents a localized
motion hypothesis estimated by the Kalman filter. Clustering serves to identify sets
of points that likely belong to the same moving object in the environment.

This task is challenging due to the sparsity and variability of the input data.
Objects may vary in size, shape, and velocity; some points may be isolated or
unreliable due to sensor noise or tracking loss. As a result, the clustering method
must be robust, flexible, and capable of handling outliers.

Several clustering techniques have been proposed in literature for similar tasks.
Among the most common are:


\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{K-Means~\cite{k_means_original}}: a partition-based algorithm that assigns points to a fixed
        number of clusters. It assumes convex cluster shapes and equal density,
        which is often not the case in real-world perception problems.
  \item \textbf{Hierarchical clustering~\cite{Hierarchical_original}}: a tree-based method that builds a
        cluster hierarchy by recursively merging or splitting groups. It does not
        require prior knowledge of the number of clusters, but has high computational
        cost and is sensitive to noise.
  \item \textbf{Mean Shift~\cite{mean_shift_original}}: a mode-seeking algorithm that clusters based on density
        peaks. It can discover arbitrarily shaped clusters, but tends to be
        computationally expensive and sensitive to bandwidth selection.
  \item \textbf{\gls{DBSCAN}~\cite{dbscan_original}}: a density-based algorithm that identifies clusters as
        connected regions of high point density. It does not require the number of
        clusters in advance and handles noise explicitly.
\end{itemize}

Given the requirements of this project: unstructured input, unknown number of
objects, and frequent outliers, DBSCAN was selected. It is well suited to segment
point clouds where object boundaries are defined by density rather than geometry.
Moreover, \gls{DBSCAN} can be extended with a custom distance function, enabling the
integration of both spatial and motion cues into the clustering process.

In this implementation, the algorithm considers two points as neighbors if they
are close in space and exhibit similar velocity vectors. This joint criterion is
more discriminative than position alone, especially in dynamic scenes where
multiple objects may temporarily overlap in 3D space.


\subsection{DBSCAN clustering}
\label{sec:DBSCANClustering}

\gls{DBSCAN} (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise) is a
non-parametric clustering algorithm introduced by Ester et al.~\cite{dbscan_original}.
It defines clusters as areas of high point density and treats sparse regions as
separators or noise. This density-based definition allows \gls{DBSCAN} to discover clusters
of arbitrary shape and to operate without requiring the number of clusters in advance.

The algorithm operates on a dataset of points and requires two parameters:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\varepsilon$: the neighborhood radius around each point
  \item $minPts$: the minimum number of points within the radius to form a dense region
\end{itemize}

The process begins by iterating over each point in the dataset:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item For a given point, its $\varepsilon$-neighborhood is computed.
  \item If the number of neighbors is greater than or equal to $minPts$, the point is
        marked as a \textit{core point}.
  \item A new cluster is initiated and the neighborhood of the core point is expanded
        recursively to include all density-connected points.
  \item If a point is not a core point and not reachable from any cluster, it is marked
        as noise.
\end{enumerate}

Two points are considered \textit{density-connected} if there exists a chain of core
points such that each is within $\varepsilon$ of the next. This enables clusters to
grow organically around dense regions and stop at natural boundaries.

Figure~\ref{fig:dbscan_clusters} illustrates the basic idea of \gls{DBSCAN}. Core points have
dense neighborhoods and initiate clusters. Border points lie within the neighborhood
of a core point but lack enough neighbors to be core points themselves. Noise points
do not belong to any cluster.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth]{figures/dbscan_clusters.png}
  \caption{Illustration of DBSCAN clustering: core, border, and noise points. Source:~\cite{dbscan_clusters}}
  \label{fig:dbscan_clusters}
\end{figure}

This structure makes \gls{DBSCAN} robust to spurious detections and suitable for real-world
scenarios with irregular object shapes. In dynamic environments, it is common for
moving objects to generate clusters of varying density. \gls{DBSCAN} handles this without
the need to fit a global model or impose strict geometric assumptions.

In our application, the algorithm is applied not in pure euclidean space, but using a
distance function that incorporates both spatial and motion similarity. The definition
of this distance and its implementation are described in the following sections.

\subsubsection{Implementation}
\label{sec:ObjectDetectionClass}

The \texttt{ObjectDetector} class is the central component responsible for identifying
dynamic objects from the 6D state field computed in the Kalman filter stage. It is
designed as a self-contained module that can be reused and evaluated independently of
the ROS2 framework. The main purpose of this class is to convert a dense field of
motion vectors into a sparse list of detected object representations suitable for
higher-level reasoning.

The class exposes a public method, \texttt{detect()}, which performs the complete
processing pipeline. The input to this method is an OpenCV matrix of type
\texttt{CV\_32FC(7)}, where each pixel encodes a 6D motion estimate and a validity
flag. The output is a list of \texttt{WorldEntity} instances, each corresponding to a
cluster of consistent motion vectors.

Internally, the pipeline consists of three sequential stages. These are described below
in detail.

\paragraph{1. CUDA-based filtering}

The first stage is responsible for identifying valid and informative motion vectors
from the full image. Given that the input may contain a large number of pixels,
including invalid or static ones, it is essential to reduce the dataset before
clustering. To achieve this efficiently, a custom CUDA kernel is used. Each thread in
the kernel processes one pixel, applying a sequence of checks:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Validity: The flag channel must indicate a valid tracked point.
  \item Motion: The magnitude of the velocity vector must exceed a minimum threshold.
  \item Depth: The 3D position must be finite and within a configured spatial range.
\end{itemize}

Points that pass all filters are copied into a device buffer. After kernel execution,
the filtered data is transferred from GPU to CPU memory. This parallel approach allows
processing large images at interactive rates, even when the proportion of valid points
is low.

\paragraph{2. DBSCAN clustering}

The second stage takes the filtered 6D points and performs density-based clustering
using \gls{DBSCAN}. This is implemented using the \texttt{mlpack} C++ library, which provides
an extensible interface for clustering with custom distance functions. In this case,
a metric combining spatial distance and velocity similarity is used. The motivation
and details of this distance function are presented in the following subsection.

\gls{DBSCAN} is configured with parameters $\varepsilon$ and $minPts$, which are specified
as ROS2 parameters. These control the local density required to initiate a cluster and
the radius used to define neighborhoods. Clusters with fewer than the minimum size are
discarded as noise. This helps remove isolated points or spurious detections that may
pass the filtering stage but do not correspond to any coherent object. \paragraph{3. Cluster-to-object conversion}

For each valid cluster found by \gls{DBSCAN}, a new \texttt{WorldEntity} object is created.
This class encapsulates the aggregate properties of the cluster, including its 3D
bounding box, average velocity, and center position. The conversion is straightforward:
all points in the cluster are passed to the constructor of the \texttt{WorldEntity},
which then computes the relevant statistics. This separation of concerns improves
modularity and simplifies testing.

The high-level flow of the detection pipeline is outlined in
Algorithm~\ref{alg:object_detector_pipeline}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Object detection pipeline (\texttt{ObjectDetector::detect})}
  \label{alg:object_detector_pipeline}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \State \textbf{Input:} 6D motion field (OpenCV matrix)
    \State \textbf{Output:} List of \texttt{WorldEntity} objects
    \State
    \State Launch CUDA kernel to extract valid motion vectors
    \State Transfer filtered points from GPU to CPU
    \If{number of valid points $<$ minimum}
      \State \Return empty list
    \EndIf
    \State Instantiate DBSCAN with custom distance function
    \State Run clustering on the filtered point set
    \ForAll{output clusters}
      \If{cluster size $<$ minimum allowed}
        \State discard as noise
      \Else
        \State construct \texttt{WorldEntity} from cluster
      \EndIf
    \EndFor
    \State \Return list of detected entities
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The figure shows the main attributes, public interface, and key internal methods used

The following section describes the custom distance function in detail.


\paragraph{Custom distance function}


The clustering stage uses a custom distance metric that combines spatial proximity
with velocity coherence, enabling the detection of objects not only based on their
location but also on their motion behavior. The implementation is provided in the
\texttt{WeightedPosVelDistance} struct defined in
\texttt{point\_distance.hpp}. Each input point is a six-dimensional vector that
encodes position \((x, y, z)\) and velocity \((v_x, v_y, v_z)\).

The distance between two points \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) is computed as:

\begin{equation}
d(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}) = w_p \cdot d_p + w_v \cdot \theta
\end{equation}

where \(w_p\) and \(w_v\) are the position and velocity weights, \(d_p\) is the
euclidean distance between the 3D positions, and \(\theta\) is the angle between
the velocity vectors of the two points.

The euclidean position distance is computed as:

\begin{equation}
d_p = \| \mathbf{p}_a - \mathbf{p}_b \|_2 = \sqrt{(x_a - x_b)^2 + (y_a - y_b)^2 + (z_a - z_b)^2}
\end{equation}

The angle \(\theta\) between velocity vectors is defined as:

\begin{equation}
\theta = \arccos\left( \frac{ \mathbf{v}_a \cdot \mathbf{v}_b }{ \| \mathbf{v}_a \| \cdot \| \mathbf{v}_b \| } \right)
\end{equation}

To ensure numerical stability, the angle is clamped to the interval \([0, \pi]\). If
either velocity vector has near-zero magnitude, the angle is set to \(\pi\), indicating
maximum dissimilarity. This design penalizes static or noisy points when compared to
moving ones, encouraging clusters to form around coherent velocity fields.

This formulation enables the algorithm to separate objects that are close in space but
move in different directions. For example, two cars driving past each other at close
distance will be assigned to different clusters due to their opposing velocity vectors.
Conversely, spatially disjoint points moving in the same direction may still be grouped
if their positions lie within the specified range and the velocity angle is small.

The distance weights \(w_p\) and \(w_v\) are passed to the constructor of the distance
functor and exposed as ROS2 parameters, allowing the clustering behavior to be tuned
at runtime. Internally, the function avoids unnecessary allocations and uses
precomputed dot products and norms to ensure efficiency during large-scale clustering.

This hybrid distance formulation provides a principled way to fuse geometric and motion
information and is a key enabler for robust clustering in dynamic environments.


\subsubsection{WorldEntity class}
\label{sec:WorldEntityClass}

Each cluster produced by the \gls{DBSCAN} algorithm is converted into a \texttt{WorldEntity}
object, which encapsulates its geometric and kinematic properties in a compact
representation. The class is initialized with a list of 3D points and corresponding
velocity vectors, from which it computes the centroid, average velocity, and an
oriented bounding box. These quantities are stored internally and accessed via public
getter methods. The centroid is computed as the arithmetic mean of all 3D positions,
and the average velocity is calculated analogously from the velocity vectors. These two
features provide a representative location and motion estimate for the object.

In addion, a bounding box of the cluster is computed using a Principal Component Analysis (PCA)~\Cite{pca_bounding_box}, which enables
an orientation-aware description of its shape. The procedure consists of the
following steps:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Center the input data by subtracting the mean position from each point.
  \item Form a \( 3 \times N \) matrix containing all centered 3D points.
  \item Compute the covariance matrix of the centered data.
  \item Solve the eigenvalue decomposition to obtain three orthogonal basis vectors.
  \item Project all points into this PCA-aligned coordinate system.
  \item Determine the minimum and maximum bounds along each principal axis.
  \item Reconstruct the eight corners of the bounding box in world coordinates by
        transforming the axis-aligned box in PCA space back to the original frame.
\end{enumerate}

This results in an oriented bounding box that tightly encloses the cluster. The three
axes of the box correspond to the directions of maximum variance in the data, and the
box dimensions reflect the spatial spread along those axes. If the number of points is
too low to perform PCA reliably, a fallback axis-aligned bounding box is computed
directly from the minimum and maximum values in each coordinate dimension.

This bounding box serves not only as a geometric representation of the object, but also for the 
computation of the criticality metric. Thanks to the bounding boxes, its not necessary anymore
to assume the objects to be point-like, but they can be represented as 3D boxes. This allows for a more accurate

In addition to geometric information, the class assigns a unique identifier to each
entity and includes utility methods for converting its contents into ROS2-compatible
messages. These include a method for generating a visualization marker and another for
publishing the entity as part of a message array. The class diagram shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:world_entity_uml} summarizes the internal structure and public
interface of the implementation.

\subsubsection{ROS2 integration}
\label{sec:ROS2IntegrationObjectDetection}

The \texttt{ObjectDetectorNode} serves as the ROS2 interface to the object detection
module. It connects the independent detection logic with the rest of the perception
pipeline and is responsible for receiving motion data, triggering clustering, and
publishing object-level representations. 

The node subscribes to a single topic, containing the 6D motion field output from the Kalman filter stage. This field is
published as a \texttt{sensor\_msgs::msg::Image} message with encoding
\texttt{CV\_32FC(7)}. Each pixel in the image stores a seven-dimensional float vector:
three values for position, three for velocity, and one flag indicating if the data pixel contains
a tracked point. 

Upon receiving a new message, the callback function \texttt{callback6dImage()} converts
the input to a \texttt{cv::Mat}, which is then passed to the detector. The
\texttt{ObjectDetector} instance processes the image and returns a list of clustered
entities. These clusters are then published in two forms: as RViz visualization markers
and as structured messages for downstream consumption.

The structured output is published on the \texttt{/object\_clusters} topic using a
custom message type \texttt{ClusteredObjectArray}. This message encapsulates all
detected objects for a given frame. It contains a header, the number of clusters, and a
vector of \texttt{ClusteredObject} messages. Each \texttt{ClusteredObject} includes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{id}: a unique integer identifier for the object
  \item \texttt{position}: a \texttt{geometry\_msgs::Point} representing the centroid
  \item \texttt{velocity}: a \texttt{geometry\_msgs::Vector3} with average motion
  \item \texttt{bb\_marker}: a \texttt{visualization\_msgs::Marker} for visualization

\section{Criticality pipeline: TTC computation}
\label{sec:CriticallityPipelineTTCComputation}

This section addresses the final stage of the perception pipeline:
the computation of Time-to-Collision (TTC) as a criticality indicator. 
TTC quantifies the temporal proximity between the ego vehicle and 
external dynamic entities, assuming a constant relative velocity. 
It is widely used in automated driving due to its simplicity, 
computational efficiency, and direct interpretability.

Beyond TTC, various criticality metrics have been proposed in the 
literature. Westhofen et al.~\cite{cricitality_metrics} provide 
a comprehensive taxonomy, grouping them into time-based, 
distance-based, probabilistic, and hybrid categories. Several of 
these metrics directly extend TTC, enriching its semantics or 
adapting it to specific contexts. Among the most relevant TTC-based metrics are the following:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Time-to-Brake (TTB)}: estimates the time remaining 
  until a braking maneuver is required to avoid collision, based 
  on a constant deceleration model. It refines TTC by considering 
  the ego braking capacity.

  \item \textbf{Time-to-Steer (TTS)}: measures the time available 
  before a steering maneuver becomes necessary to avoid an 
  obstacle. It introduces lateral dynamics and is particularly 
  relevant in evasive scenarios.

  \item \textbf{Time-to-React (TTR)}: incorporates human or system 
  reaction time into the TTC estimation, often used in driver 
  monitoring and shared control contexts.

  \item \textbf{Time-to-Avoidance (TTA)}: models the time available 
  until an avoidance maneuver (brake or steer) becomes ineffective. 
  It compares TTC with the time needed to complete the avoidance 
  maneuver successfully.

  \item \textbf{Acceleration-based metrics (AM)}: estimate the 
  required acceleration to avoid a collision within a given time 
  window, assuming dynamic constraints. These are useful in 
  risk-aware motion planning.
\end{itemize}

All these metrics maintain a direct conceptual dependency on TTC, 
which serves as the temporal reference for evaluating risk. 
Figure~\ref{fig:ttc_metric_network} shows a dependency graph of 
commonly used criticality metrics, with TTC at its core.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/criticallityMetrics.png}
  \caption{Dependency graph of criticality metrics based on TTC. Adapted from~\cite{cricitality_metrics}}\label{fig:ttc_metric_network}
\end{figure}

While TTC is often computed under the assumption of point-like 
agents, this approximation neglects object geometry and orientation. 
In structured urban environments, this simplification can lead to 
under- or overestimation of risk, especially in dense traffic or 
intersection scenarios.

A more accurate strategy considers object dimensions through oriented 
bounding boxes. Jiménez et al.~\cite{improvedTTC} propose 
a method that models each vehicle as a rectangular body and evaluates 
potential collisions by tracking corner-to-edge interactions over time. 
Their formulation handles various motion configurations and computes 
the TTC based on geometric overlap rather than idealized point 
intersections.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{figures/improvedTTC.png}
  \caption{Improved TTC computation based on rectangle-based vehicles. Adapted from~\cite{improvedTTC}}\label{fig:improvedTTC}
\end{figure}

Such approaches could be integrated into future iterations of the 
criticality pipeline, leveraging the bounding box information already 
available from the object detection stage (Section~\ref{sec:WorldEntityClass}). 
This would enable more realistic contact prediction and improve the 
robustness of the TTC computation under complex spatial arrangements.

\subsection{Time-to-Collision metric}
\label{sec:TTCMetricDefinition}

After introducing the conceptual role of TTC in the broader context of 
criticality estimation, this subsection presents a more detailed 
mathematical and algorithmic formulation of the metric. 

The objective is to formalize its computation, analyze the assumptions 
underlying its definition, and discuss how it is adapted and extended 
in this work to support trajectory-aware reasoning.

The Time-to-Collision (TTC) is a fundamental metric for estimating 
collision risk in autonomous driving scenarios. It expresses the 
time remaining until a potential collision occurs, assuming that 
both agents continue moving with constant velocity. Its main 
advantage lies in its simplicity, low computational requirements, 
and direct interpretability.

In its basic formulation, the Time-to-Collision (TTC) represents the 
time it would take for two objects to collide if they continue moving 
at constant velocity. It is computed as the current distance between 
the two agents divided by the difference in their velocities projected 
onto the line connecting them.

Let $\mathbf{p}_{\text{rel}} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ be the relative position 
vector from the ego vehicle to the object, and 
$\mathbf{v}_{\text{rel}} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ the relative velocity between 
them. The TTC is then defined as:

\begin{equation}
  \text{TTC} = \frac{ \| \mathbf{p}_{\text{rel}} \| }{ - \mathbf{v}_{\text{rel}}^\top 
  \cdot \hat{\mathbf{p}}_{\text{rel}} }
  \label{eq:ttc_twist}
\end{equation}

Here, $\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{\text{rel}} = \mathbf{p}_{\text{rel}} / 
\| \mathbf{p}_{\text{rel}} \|$ is the unit vector pointing from the ego 
vehicle towards the object. The denominator in 
Equation~\eqref{eq:ttc_twist} corresponds to the projection of the 
relative velocity onto this direction vector, i.e., the component of 
$\mathbf{v}_{\text{rel}}$ that is aligned with the separation vector.
This projection represents how quickly the distance between the two 
agents is decreasing along the line of sight. If this projection is 
negative, it indicates that the object is approaching the ego vehicle. 
A positive projection implies divergence, in which case TTC is 
undefined or infinite.

Thus, the TTC can be interpreted as:

\begin{equation}
  \text{TTC} =
  \frac{\mathit{distance}}{\mathit{rate\ of\ approach}} 
  = \frac{\| \mathbf{p}_{\text{rel}} \|}{- \mathbf{v}_{\text{rel}}^\top
  \cdot \hat{\mathbf{p}}_{\text{rel}}}
\end{equation}

The numerator is the earlier euclidean distance between the object and 
the ego vehicle, while the denominator is the closing velocity. 

This geometric interpretation is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:ttc_twist_diagram}, 
where the TTC is calculated as the ratio between the relative 
distance and the rate at which that distance shortens along the 
collision axis.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/ttc_twist.png}
  \caption{Geometric interpretation of time-to-collision}
  \label{fig:ttc_twist_diagram}
\end{figure}


Equation~\eqref{eq:ttc_twist} assumes rectilinear motion at constant 
velocity, with both agents approximated as point masses. 
Moreover, the formulation implicitly assumes that a collision occurs 
when the object reaches the current position of the ego-vehicle, ignoring 
their respective shapes and orientations.

To overcome some of these limitations, a second approach is 
implemented based on forward simulation over the ego vehicle's 
planned trajectory (figure~\ref{fig:ttc_path_diagram}). Let $\mathcal{P} = \{ \mathbf{p}_1, \dots, 
\mathbf{p}_N \}$ denote a discrete sequence of future ego poses, 
sampled at a fixed rate $1/\Delta t$, and let $\mathbf{o}_0$ and 
$\mathbf{v}_o$ be the current position and velocity of the object.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/ttc_path.png}
  \caption{Time-to-collision with ego path consideration}
  \label{fig:ttc_path_diagram}
\end{figure}

Assuming constant object velocity, its future position at time 
$t_i = i \cdot \Delta t$ is predicted as:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{o}_i = \mathbf{o}_0 + i \cdot \Delta t \cdot \mathbf{v}_o
\end{equation}

The TTC is then defined as the first $t_i$ such that the distance 
between the ego vehicle and the object falls below a collision 
threshold $d_{\text{th}}$:

\begin{equation}
  \| \mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{o}_i \| < d_{\text{th}}
\end{equation}

The threshold $d_{\text{th}}$ accounts for the physical dimensions 
of the ego vehicle and a minimal buffer. A typical value ranges 
from 0.5 to 1.5 meters, depending on system resolution and safety 
requirements.

This method leverages existing trajectory information and enables 
a more realistic estimation of collision likelihood in structured 
environments. It is particularly relevant when the ego vehicle is 
navigating a curve or executing complex maneuvers where the 
twist-based approximation becomes inaccurate.

Despite this improvement, both approaches treat vehicles as 
dimensionless points. In practice, real objects occupy space and 
have non-negligible extent. Ignoring object geometry may lead 
to conservative or optimistic estimates, especially when bounding 
boxes partially overlap without their centers being aligned.

To address this, an extended TTC formulation could incorporate 
bounding box geometry into the distance computation. Jiménez 
et al.~\cite{improvedTTC} propose a method where each 
vehicle is modeled as a rectangle, and TTC is calculated based on 
the earliest contact between corners and edges. Their approach 
enumerates the possible collision configurations and accounts 
for both relative motion and orientation.

In the current pipeline, each object is already associated with an 
oriented bounding box derived from clustering (see 
Section~\ref{sec:WorldEntityClass}). This enables a future 
extension where the collision threshold $d_{\text{th}}$ is replaced 
by geometric contact prediction between the bounding box of the ego-vehicle and the one of the object. This enhancement would 
lead to more accurate criticality assessments, particularly in 
dense urban scenarios or during turning maneuvers.

\subsection{Implementation: TTC detection module}
\label{sec:TTCImplementation}

The TTC estimation described above is implemented as a dedicated 
ROS2 node named ttc\_calculator\_node inside the ttc\_calculator package. This component 
serves as the bridge between the object-level output of the 
perception pipeline and the criticality estimation required for 
downstream decision-making modules.

The node operates in two modes: twist-based or path-based TTC, 
depending on a configurable parameter. In both cases, it receives 
as input the list of detected objects, each represented as a 
\texttt{ClusteredObject} custom message containing position, velocity, and 
bounding box information. The output is a list of the same objects, 
each annotated with a TTC estimate.

The node subscribes to the following input topics:
\texttt{/object\_clusters} (\texttt{ClusteredObjectArray}), which 
contains the list of detected objects; 
\texttt{/ego\_twist} (\texttt{geometry\_msgs::Twist}), which 
provides the linear velocity of the ego vehicle; and optionally 
\texttt{/ego\_path} (\texttt{nav\_msgs::Path}), which defines the 
planned trajectory in path-based mode.



The output topics are:
\texttt{/object\_ttc}, publishing the list of objects with updated 
TTC values;
\texttt{/min\_ttc}, containing the minimum TTC observed in the 
current frame;
and \texttt{/ego\_marker}, which publishes RViz markers for 
visualizing the ego vehicle geometry and velocity vector.


When a new object array is received, the node computes the TTC 
for each object based on the selected mode. In path mode, the future 
position of the object is compared to the ego trajectory as described 
in Section~\ref{sec:TTCMetricDefinition}. In twist mode, the relative 
velocity is projected onto the direction vector connecting the agents. 
The result is stored in the \texttt{ttc} field of the output message. 
The minimum TTC value is also computed and published separately.

The node is fully configurable through parameters. These include 
topic names for input and output; the dimensions of the ego vehicle 
(\texttt{ego\_length}, \texttt{ego\_width}, \texttt{ego\_height}); the 
reference frame (\texttt{frame\_id}); and flags for enabling path-based 
mode (\texttt{use\_path}) and RViz visualization 
(\texttt{publish\_ego\_marker}). All parameters can be defined via 
a launch file, allowing flexible integration into larger systems.

The node optionally publishes visualization markers in the 
\texttt{camera\_optical\_frame}, showing the ego vehicle as a green 
cube and its velocity as a red arrow. These markers provide an 
intuitive representation of motion in the scene and assist in debugging 
and validation.

The design ensures real-time operation, with each TTC computation 
performed independently for each object. This allows for future 
parallelization and the integration of more advanced models, such as 
bounding box contact estimation or probabilistic motion prediction.

\chapter{Experiments and Results}
\label{chap:ExperimentsAndResults}

This chapter presents the experimental validation of the perception and 
criticality pipelines developed in this thesis. The evaluation addresses three 
main goals. First, it assesses the suitability of the selected stereo hardware 
for 6D motion estimation, including the calibration procedures required for 
integration with the perception pipeline. In case of insufficient performance, 
alternative hardware is evaluated and re-integrated accordingly. Second, the 
computational performance of the full pipeline is analyzed to determine whether 
real-time constraints can be fulfilled on representative embedded platforms. 
Third, the output of the deterministic system is compared against the learned 
perception stack currently deployed on the EDGAR research vehicle, with a 
particular focus on object detection quality and Time-to-Collision estimation.

All experiments are designed to evaluate compliance with the functional and 
technical requirements introduced in 
Chapter~\ref{sec:RequirementsForTheThesis}. These include stereo-only sensing, 
semantic independence, real-time capability, modular implementation, and 
robustness under partial occlusion. Additional criteria involve 6D motion 
tracking, consistent egomotion compensation, clustering of dynamic points, and 
runtime computation of criticality metrics. The objective is not only to validate 
the proposed design, but also to quantify its reliability, accuracy, and 
deployability under realistic operational conditions.

The remainder of this chapter is structured as follows. Section~\ref{sec:CameraHardware} 
describes the camera hardware evaluation and calibration procedures. Section~\ref{sec:PerceptionEvaluation} covers the experiment 
design with the final setup as well as the actual performed tests. Section~\ref{sec:PerformanceBenchmarking} presents a runtime performance analysis, respectively. Finally, 
Section~\ref{sec:ResultsSummary} summarizes the main findings of this Chapter.

\section{Camera hardware selection and calibration}\label{sec:CameraHardware}

The perception pipeline developed in this thesis is based exclusively on 
stereo vision. Consequently, selecting a suitable camera system was a 
critical initial step. Rather than introducing external hardware, the 
evaluation started with the camera systems already available on the 
EDGAR research platform. These included: (i) a pair of Framos D455e 
depth cameras, (ii) six Basler acA1920\-50gc cameras mounted for 
surround view and long-range sensing, and (iii) a set of fisheye cameras 
dedicated to vehicle teleoperation.

Each system met the integration and software compatibility requirements 
of EDGAR, such as ROS2 support and Precision Time Protocol (PTP) 
synchronization. However, their suitability for stereo-based depth 
estimation varied significantly. Table~\ref{tab:camera_specs} summarizes 
the most relevant technical characteristics for stereo vision evaluation.

The long-range Basler setup, equipped with 16\,mm lenses, was designed 
for distant object detection, such as traffic signs. However, its narrow 
field of view and lack of stereo configuration made it unsuitable for this 
application. The surround-view system, composed of six Basler cameras 
with 6\,mm and 4.7\,mm lenses, provided wide-angle coverage but was 
intended for monocular perception. The cameras were mounted at 
non-parallel orientations around the vehicle and would require complex 
extrinsic calibration to enable stereo vision. Furthermore, the use of 
fisheye lenses violated the rectilinear geometry assumptions of the 
disparity computation module.

The Framos D455e camera was ultimately selected for the first phase of 
pipeline development. It integrates a stereo pair with a 95\,mm baseline 
and computes depth maps on board using active infrared projection. The 
camera offers VGA resolution at 30\,Hz, includes an IMU, and is 
natively compatible with ROS2. These characteristics allowed rapid 
integration and made it possible to collect initial datasets without 
extensive driver development.

The goal of this phase was not geometric calibration of the sensor 
itself, but rather the tuning of internal parameters in the perception 
pipeline. In particular, the system required calibration of:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the observation noise of the Kalman filter, including depth and 
        optical flow uncertainty;
  \item the covariance of the egomotion transform;
  \item filtering thresholds for optical flow;
  \item and parameters for motion consistency and point reinitialization.
\end{itemize}

A series of static experiments were designed and executed to collect 
the necessary data. These included synthetic motion generation 
through alpha-angle tests and depth range validation using planar 
objects at known distances. The controlled nature of the setup 
allowed for repeatability and easier parameter tuning through offline 
inspection and visualization in RViz.

The data and methodology of this calibration phase are described in 
Section~\ref{sec:FramosCalibration}. The system limitations 
encountered when the camera was tested in dynamic outdoor 
conditions are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:FramosLimitations}.

\subsection{Calibration of the perception pipeline}\label{sec:FramosCalibration}

The parameters of the perception pipeline were calibrated offline using 
the datasets acquired in Session~1 and Session~2. The goal was to adjust 
the internal noise models and detection thresholds to match the 
characteristics of the Framos stereo camera and improve the accuracy 
and stability of the resulting 6D motion estimates.

The calibration followed a progressive approach, starting from the tuning 
of sensor-specific filters and proceeding to more global parameters that 
control temporal fusion and object-level reasoning.

\paragraph{Optical flow module}

high-frequency noise or flow discontinuities.

A parameter sweep was performed on the classical Farnebäck method, 
with visual feedback in RViz overlaid on test trajectories from the 
alpha-angle dataset. The final configuration represents a trade-off 
between detection in low texture regions, detection of small displacements and the detectoin

\paragraph{Kalman filter module}

The Kalman filter forms the core of the perception pipeline, enabling 
state estimation of individual 3D points over time. Its accuracy and 
robustness are highly dependent on the tuning of its covariance matrices, 
as well as on geometric filtering parameters that determine which 
measurements are accepted into the tracking process.

Calibration was performed in stages. First, the observation noise parameters 
were adjusted using static recordings, where the subject remained within a 
controlled region. These include the depth and optical flow covariances, which 
determine the reliability of each measurement. Next, the system noise parameters 
were tuned to stabilize the 6D velocity estimation, particularly in the alpha-angle 
dataset. Finally, the egomotion uncertainty values were adjusted using the 
dynamic recordings, allowing the system to adapt to camera motion in real-world 
conditions.

The filter also includes geometric constraints to reject measurements outside 
the desired spatial region. These include minimum and maximum valid depths, 
as well as vertical height thresholds that discard ground or ceiling clutter.

\subsection{Results, limitations and motivation for replacement}\label{sec:FramosLimitations}

Despite the successful integration and calibration of the Framos D455e stereo 
camera, the experiments revealed critical limitations that significantly hindered 
its suitability for mobile perception tasks.

The most prominent issue was related to depth range and noise. Although the 
theoretical range of the sensor was specified as 6 meters, in practice, depth values 
could be obtained up to approximately 10 meters. However, in outdoor conditions, 
the depth image became dominated by static noise beyond 6--7 meters, rendering 
it ineffective for object detection. This degradation was attributed to the use of 
infrared stereo matching, which is known to suffer under natural sunlight.

Across all distances, the depth image exhibited a high level of baseline noise, 
which increased with range. This made object contours hard to distinguish and 
reduced the spatial consistency of measurements. Attempts to mitigate these 
effects through noise filtering and Kalman filter calibration had limited success. 
Even with tuned parameters, the state estimation quality remained suboptimal, 
particularly in dynamic scenes.

Another significant limitation was the restricted effective detection range during 
vehicle motion. While a 6-meter range might be marginally acceptable in static 
scenarios, it proved insufficient under driving conditions. At speeds typical for 
urban operation, the time between first detection and potential collision became 
too short to allow reliable tracking and response. This severely undermined the 
feasibility of using the Framos sensor in a real-time criticality estimation context.

Given these constraints, it was concluded that the Framos D455e did not meet the 
minimum performance requirements of the system. In particular, the perception 
pipeline required robust detection of moving objects at distances of 10--20 meters 
to ensure sufficient anticipation during motion. The noise profile and depth 
limitations of the Framos made it unfit for this purpose.

These findings motivated the search for a replacement stereo camera with higher 
range, lower noise, and better outdoor performance. The next section presents the 
criteria and rationale behind the selection of the Carnegie Robotics S30 sensor.


\subsection{Selection of the Carnegie S30 stereo camera}\label{sec:CarnegieSelection}

The perception pipeline initially developed for the EDGAR research vehicle was 
designed around the Framos D455e stereo camera. While this sensor allowed rapid 
prototyping and testing under static indoor conditions, its limitations became 
evident during outdoor operation with moving ego vehicle. 

In particular, excessive depth noise, limited 
detection range, and susceptibility to sunlight rendered the Framos unsuitable 
for reliable object tracking in dynamic driving scenarios. These issues are 
discussed in detail in Section~\ref{sec:FramosLimitations}.

To address these shortcomings, a systematic evaluation of alternative stereo vision 
systems was initiated. The objective was to identify a sensor that could reliably 
detect moving objects at distances of 15\-30\,m under real-world conditions, while 
remaining compatible with the existing software infrastructure. In addition, the 
new sensor needed to offer deterministic data output, robust synchronization 
features, and mechanical resilience suitable for long-term deployment in a 
research vehicle.

This section details the criteria used in the selection process, the evaluated 
candidates, and the rationale for ultimately choosing the Carnegie Robotics 
Multisense S30 camera.

\paragraph{Technical requirements}

To ensure compatibility with the EDGAR software and hardware architecture~\cite{edgar_hardware}, 
a set of mandatory requirements was defined:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Driver compatibility:} ROS2 Humble support, 
        with active maintenance and documentation.
  \item \textbf{Timestamp synchronization:} Support for IEEE 1588 PTP to ensure 
        temporal alignment with other sensors.
  \item \textbf{Depth range:} Reliable stereo depth beyond 15\,m in outdoor settings, 
        sufficient to detect and track objects before critical TTC thresholds are reached
  \item \textbf{On-board processing:} To enable local computation and reduce CPU load 
        on the ego vehicle main computer.
  \item \textbf{Environmental sealing:} Resistance to water, dust, and sunlight exposure, 
        with at least IP65 protection rating.
  \item \textbf{Communication:} Ethernet connectivity (Gigabit) for high-bandwidth 
        and low-latency data transmission.
  \item \textbf{Field of view:} Minimum horizontal coverage of 90  to monitor 
        the vehicle’s forward sector. 
  \item \textbf{Frame rate:} At least 20\,Hz to maintain sufficient temporal resolution 
        for 6D motion estimation.
\end{itemize}

Two optional features were also considered advantageous:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Power over Ethernet (PoE):} To simplify wiring and reduce connector count.
  \item \textbf{Cost limit:} Total acquisition cost, including necessary accessories, 
        below €3000.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Parameter tuning and egomotion compensation}

The calibration process began with the tuning of the measurement 
model in the Kalman filter. Compared to the Framos D455e, the S30 
camera provides significantly lower noise in depth and image signals. 
As a result, the covariance values associated with depth and optical 
flow measurements were lowered to reflect the improved sensor 
quality. The observation noise introduced by the egomotion module 
was also reduced based on visual inspection of tracked features in 
static scenes.

The second stage focused on the system model. The process noise 
parameters controlling the 6D velocity propagation were adapted to 
match the smoother measurements obtained with the new camera. 
In particular, the variance along the vertical axis ($\sigma^2_z$) was 
reduced to prevent unstable motion artifacts caused by minor 
depth fluctuations in the road surface.

To further improve robustness in dynamic scenes, an ego-motion 
compensation gain factor was introduced. This factor scales the 
velocity of the ego vehicle before subtracting it from each tracked 
point. A gain of 1.25 was found to best suppress residual motion 
artefacts without introducing overcompensation. This adjustment 
proved essential to reduce ghost vectors and spurious motion 
trails in scenes with strong ego movement.

\subsubsection{Filtering of invalid motion vectors}

During early tests with the S30 sensor, it was observed that static objects 
or noisy regions in the background often yielded spurious motion vectors 
pointing away from the ego vehicle. These artefacts, typically caused by 
imperfect depth projection or minor image misalignments, could lead to 
false positive detections during the clustering stage.

To mitigate this issue, a new filter was introduced in the object detection 
module. This filter excludes points whose velocity vectors in the camera 
optical axis (Z-direction) exceed a configurable receding threshold. 
The rationale behind this strategy is that objects moving away from the 
vehicle do not contribute to immediate collision risk, and are often 
associated with unstable measurements in low-texture or noisy regions.

The filtering logic was implemented as a CUDA kernel to allow efficient 
parallel processing of large point sets in the 6D output array. The kernel 
scans each vector and discards those with Z-velocity above the configured 
limit. The final implementation is shown in 
Listing~\ref{lst:receding_kernel}.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=c++,caption={CUDA kernel to filter 
receding motion vectors.},label={lst:receding_kernel}]
__global__ void recFilterPointsKernel(
  const float* data, float* filtered_data,
  int total_points, int* valid_count, float rec_th) 
{
  int idx = blockIdx.x * blockDim.x + threadIdx.x;
  if (idx >= total_points) return;

  int input_offset = idx * 6;  
  float speed_z = data[input_offset + 5];  
  if (speed_z <= rec_th) 
  {
    int output_idx = atomicAdd(valid_count, 1);
    for (int i = 0; i < 6; ++i) {
      filtered_data[output_idx * 6 + i] = data[input_offset + i];
    }
  }
}
\end{lstlisting}

The kernel is launched with a standard grid configuration and synchronised 
after execution. The `receding\_speed\_thresh` parameter, set to 0.3\,m/s, 
was empirically tuned to balance between rejecting spurious background 
vectors and preserving valid trajectories of slow-moving objects.

This filter significantly reduced false positives in static scenes and 
improved the visual coherence of object clusters in dynamic conditions.

Table~\ref{tab:carnegie_detector_params}.

    \texttt{eps}                   & 0.5 \\
    \texttt{minPts}                & 5 \\
    \texttt{pos\_weight}           & 1.0 \\
    \texttt{vel\_weight}           & 0.05 \\
    \texttt{vel\_threshold}        & 0.5 \\
    \texttt{receding\_speed\_thresh} & 0.3 \\

vectors.

absence of ego-motion:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Range test:} A person walks towards the vehicle from 
        20\,m. This measures the maximum usable detection range.
  \item \textbf{Lateral motion test:} A person walks laterally across 
        the field of view at a fixed 8\,m distance, evaluating stability 
        of motion tracking.
  \item \textbf{Angle test:} A person approaches the vehicle 
        diagonally from a point at 8\,m distance and 2.6\,m lateral 
        offset, creating an 18$^\circ$ trajectory. The test checks 
        directional sensitivity of velocity estimation.
\end{itemize}

The following sections describe each test in detail, including the
experimental setup, the results obtained, and a qualitative analysis of
the perception performance.

pipeline in the presence of ego-motion, complex object interactions, and

and tracking of moving objects, but also the reliability of estimated 
time-to-collision (TTC) values across various types of interactions.

To allow a fair comparison with Autoware, both pipelines were executed 
in parallel under identical conditions. The evaluation setup consisted of:

\begin{itemize}
  \item The stereo-based perception and criticality pipelines, running 
        as ROS2 nodes in a dedicated container.
  \item A secondary container named \texttt{criticality\_pipeline\_autoware}, 
        which launched a wrapper for the Autoware perception detected 
        objects topic, and a second instance of the developed TTC 
        calculator node.
\end{itemize}

node was developed. This node subscribes to 
\texttt{/perception/object\_recognition/objects} from Autoware and 
transforms each message into the custom object message format expected

module could be reused across both pipelines, guaranteeing a valid and 
consistent comparison.

TTC estimates, which indicate that no imminent collision was predicted by 
the pipeline at that frame.

\subsection{Summary and insights}

The evaluation results demonstrate that the stereo-based perception 
pipeline is capable of reliably detecting and tracking dynamic objects 
in a wide range of real-world scenarios. The estimated velocity vectors 
are consistent, and the clustering logic based on Kalman output 
proves robust under both static and dynamic conditions. Time-to-collision 
(TTC) estimates are generally interpretable and responsive, even 
in short-range situations.

The most critical limitation identified is the occurrence of false positives 
in dynamic scenes. These are primarily attributed to residual ego-motion 
effects not fully compensated by the Kalman-based prediction model. 
The root cause is believed to lie in the limitations of the optical flow 
estimation, particularly under fast rotations or image blur. While 
alternative visual odometry sources were tested (including Autoware 
odometry), the results did not improve significantly, suggesting that 
the issue is not due to drift but to inaccuracies in the short-term 
relative motion between consecutive frames.

In contrast, the clustering module consistently grouped coherent motion 
vectors into distinct objects, even under partial occlusions. The TTC estimation module, in turn, provided stable outputs 
closely tied to the motion and geometry of each cluster.

Comparison with Autoware reveals key differences in system design. 
While Autoware benefits from 360-degree LiDAR coverage and longer 
detection range, its velocity initialization is often insufficient for 
short-term TTC estimation. Moreover, its high object count frequently 
includes background elements or static objects, which can dilute the 
criticality assessment. In contrast, the stereo-based pipeline, although 
more range-limited, offers high resolution in the near field and does 
not depend on pretrained models or semantic classification. This makes 
it better suited for detecting novel or unstructured agents in close-range 
urban scenarios.

Finally, the system demonstrated robustness against partial occlusions, 
e.g., when multiple pedestrians crossed paths. Motion estimation 
remained stable and individual clusters were preserved. These results 
confirm the viability of a purely geometry-based perception approach 
for criticality estimation in constrained sensor setups. Nonetheless, the 
current implementation could benefit from enhancements in optical 
flow estimation. In particular, integrating learning-based augmentation 
modules could help suppress flow inconsistencies in challenging scenes, 
such as fast turns or low-contrast areas, ultimately improving both 
tracking stability and false positive rejection.

\section{Runtime and performance benchmarking}\label{sec:PerformanceBenchmarking}

This section presents a runtime and resource usage evaluation of the
developed stereo-based perception pipeline. The goal is to assess its
feasibility for real-time deployment on embedded platforms.

Performance measurements were conducted on two platforms: a personal
workstation and the EDGAR onboard computer. On the PC, tests were
performed offline using pre-recorded rosbags. On EDGAR, the system
ran in real-time, processing sensor data as it arrived.

The evaluation focuses on the most computationally intensive modules
of the pipeline:

\subsection{Resource usage analysis}\label{sec:ResourceUsage}

To evaluate the computational cost of the perception pipeline,
three main metrics were measured: CPU load, RAM usage, and GPU memory
consumption. The evaluation focused on the core modules of the system:
optical flow, Kalman filtering, object clustering, and visual odometry.

A custom Python script (\texttt{ressource\_monitor.py}) was used for
data collection. It is located in the
\texttt{non\_ros/performance\_testing/ressource\_analysis} directory,
and relies on the \texttt{psutil} and \texttt{pynvml} libraries to
record per-process statistics and \texttt{bokeh} to plot the results.

CPU usage is reported as the sum across all cores, so values above
100\% are expected in multithreaded workloads. RAM is measured in MiB.
For GPU, only memory usage was recorded, as accurately capturing per process compute
utilization on Linux systems with the NVIDIA driver was not possible.

Each run lasted approximately two minutes and covered a realistic
perception scenario. Logging started once the system reached steady
state, to avoid initialization effects.

Results are presented separately for the desktop PC and the EDGAR
onboard computer.

\subsection{Callback execution profiling}\label{sec:CallbackAnalysis}

This subsection presents the per-callback execution time for the core 
nodes of the perception pipeline. Callback duration is a key indicator 
for evaluating real-time performance, especially under high sensor 
frequency or embedded hardware.

Measurements were collected using the \texttt{ros2\_tracing} suite. A 
custom script, \texttt{ros2\_tracing\_container.sh}, was used to 
automatically launch a tracing session inside the selected Docker 
container. Once completed, the recorded data is automatically analyzed using 
\texttt{ros2\_callback\_analysis.py}, which extracts and processes the 
callback durations.

For each callback, two plots are shown: one representing the callback 
execution time over the trace duration, and one showing the histogram 
of durations. This combination allows us to assess both the real-time 
variability and the typical runtime of each module.

All durations are reported in milliseconds. Each plot reflects only the 
user-space callback execution time, excluding scheduling latency or 
inter-process overhead. Its important to note, that these durations do not reflect the total processing time for 
each node, since they do not include some other operations like the publishing time.

The callbacks analyzed are:


\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Optical flow}: subscriber to the left grayscale image
  \item \textbf{Visual odometry}: synchronized callbacks for rectified color and depth images
  \item \textbf{Kalman filter}: subscriber to optical flow messages
  \item \textbf{Object detector}: subscriber to the 6D estimation output
\end{itemize}

The first set of measurements was obtained by running the full

rosbag. This ensured deterministic input and avoided variability due to 
sensor latency or system load fluctuations.

\subsection{Summary and insights}\label{subsec:SummaryAndInsights}

The performance evaluation of the perception pipeline focused on two 
complementary aspects: resource usage (CPU, GPU, RAM) and per-callback 
execution times. Both offline (desktop PC) and real-time (EDGAR onboard) 
scenarios were analyzed, providing a comprehensive view of the system's 
behavior under realistic conditions.

CPU and memory profiling revealed that the \texttt{optical\_flow\_co} 
and \texttt{kalman\_filter\_n} nodes were the most computationally 
demanding. On the desktop PC, both exhibited sustained CPU usage above 
100\,\% due to multithreaded execution. On EDGAR, the optical flow node 
reached an average of 133\,\%, reflecting its high processing demand. 
GPU memory usage remained moderate across all nodes, typically below 
400\,MB, with peaks in the optical flow module. RAM usage was highest in 
the Kalman filter, reaching up to 1.4\,GB, as expected due to the storage 
of multiple tracked states and covariances.

Callback execution times confirmed that most components operate within 
real-time constraints. The optical flow and visual odometry (depth) 
callbacks were the most time-critical, with average runtimes near 
50--60\,ms and occasional spikes. These results indicate tight timing 
margins and motivate further optimization or parallelization. Other 
callbacks, such as visual odometry (color) and object detector, 
maintained consistently low durations, below 5--15\,ms. The Kalman 
filter callback showed a bimodal distribution, with durations near zero 
for sparse frames and longer runtimes when many points were updated. 
This adaptive behavior helps maintain responsiveness under varying 
conditions.

To validate runtime frequency, the \texttt{ros2 topic hz} tool was used 
to measure the output rate of the final topic 
\texttt{/multisense/perception\_pipeline/output\_6d}. During multiple 
driving tests on EDGAR, the system consistently produced outputs above 
12\,Hz, which satisfies the 10\,Hz requirement defined in 
Section~\ref{sec:RequirementsForTheThesis}. This confirms the real-time viability of the 
pipeline in deployment conditions.

\section{Summary of experimental results}\label{sec:ResultsSummary}

The experiments conducted throughout this chapter confirm that the perception and
criticality estimation pipeline meets the majority of the design requirements
outlined in Section~\ref{sec:RequirementsForTheThesis}. The following paragraphs
review each requirement individually, based on data collected during sensor
evaluation, static and dynamic tests, and performance benchmarking.

\textbf{1. Real-time performance:} The complete pipeline achieves a processing
frequency above 12\,Hz on the EDGAR onboard computer, thereby satisfying the
real-time requirement of 10\,Hz. Although some callbacks occasionally approach
the frame deadline, the system consistently operates within real-time
constraints, supported by CUDA-accelerated stereo processing and multithreaded
Kalman filtering.


\textbf{2. Stereo-only sensing:} All perception and estimation stages rely
exclusively on stereo images from the Carnegie Robotics S30. No LiDAR, radar,
or monocular modules are used. The earlier Framos-based pipeline was also stereo,
but its limited depth range and outdoor noise sensitivity prompted the switch
to the Carnegie platform. The new system also complies with the maximum budget of 
3000\,EUR.

\textbf{3. Semantic independence:} Object detection is entirely class-agnostic,
based on clustering of coherent 6D motion vectors. No semantic segmentation,
training data, or object labels are used. This allows the system to detect and
track previously unseen objects, in contrast to Autoware, which depends on
pretrained neural networks and semantic categories.

\textbf{4. Egomotion compensation:} A visual odometry-based module transforms
point trajectories into a consistent camera-centric frame. A tunable compensation
gain and receding vector filter increase robustness. However, sharp turns and
sudden acceleration still introduce transient estimation errors in some
scenarios.

\textbf{5. 6D output:} The core Kalman filter maintains per-point estimates of
3D position and velocity, enabling consistent 6D output across time. These
estimates were validated qualitatively in Figures~\ref{fig:lateral_motion_1},
\ref{fig:angle_test}, and others. With the Carnegie S30, sufficient depth
range and temporal stability was achieved to support this feature reliably.

\textbf{6. Clustering:} The object detector groups dynamic points into spatially
and kinematically coherent clusters. A receding motion filter is used to reject
unstable points. While some large objects may be split into multiple clusters,
this behavior favors robustness and avoids premature over-grouping.

\textbf{7. Criticality estimation:} A dedicated module computes time-to-collision
(TTC) for each detected object based on relative velocity and the ego trajectory.
This metric responds well to changes in scene configuration and provides
interpretable estimates in all evaluated test cases. In short-range scenarios,
it often outperforms the risk outputs provided by Autoware.

\textbf{8. Modular ROS2 architecture:} Each processing stage is implemented as
an independent ROS2 package. This structure supported parallel development, facilitated testing,
and allowed for modular integration or replacement of components such as
depth estimation and criticality analysis.

\textbf{9. Robustness to occlusion:} The Kalman tracker and clustering logic
enable the system to maintain object identities through temporary occlusions,
e.g., when two pedestrians cross paths in opposite directions. Occlusion handling
was particularly successful in Scenario~4, where overlapping
motion patterns were resolved without major drift or track loss.

\textbf{10. Portability and deployment:} The full perception stack is packaged
as two Docker containers and deployed on the EDGAR research vehicle. It integrates
with the onboard sensor infrastructure and operates directly on live input
from the Carnegie S30, producing consistent object detections and risk metrics
in real-time conditions.

In summary, the stereo-based system developed in this thesis satisfies the
primary requirements for geometry-driven motion perception and runtime risk
analysis in teleoperation scenarios. Compared to the initial Framos-based
prototype, it delivers improved depth range, robustness, and modularity. While
semantic-free operation remains a distinguishing strength over learned systems
such as Autoware, future work should improve egomotion handling and reduce
false positives in highly dynamic environments.

\chapter{Summary and Conclusion}\label{chap:SummaryAndConclusion}

This thesis presented the design, implementation, and evaluation of a stereo-based 
perception pipeline for motion-aware environment understanding in automated 
vehicles. The motivation for this work arises from the increasing relevance of 
teleoperation as a fallback mechanism in autonomous systems and the necessity of redundant
system in autonomous vehicles. 
During disengagements or unstructured scenarios, learning-based perception stacks may fail or produce 
unreliable outputs. In such cases, deterministic geometry-based pipelines can offer 
complementary capabilities, enhancing overall system robustness.

The primary objective was to create a fully modular system that relies only on 
stereo vision and does not require semantic labels, training data, or prior object 
models. The pipeline should be able to detect and track moving objects in 3D, 
estimate their 6D motion (position and velocity), and compute criticality metrics 
such as time-to-collision (TTC) at runtime. All components were implemented in 
C++ and ROS2, designed for deployment on the EDGAR research vehicle.

The methodology consisted of two main modules: a perception stack based on 
stereo depth, optical flow, and recursive Kalman filtering; and a criticality 
estimation module that computes TTC values using the relative motion between the 
ego vehicle and detected clusters. 

The implementation leveraged parallel processing through OpenMP and CUDA 
acceleration for real-time performance. All components were encapsulated different docker containers
as explained in~\ref{sec:SystemArchitecture}, ensuring reproducibility and ease of deployment. Extensive 
experiments were conducted to validate the system under static and dynamic 
conditions, with detailed comparison the learning based approach included in the Autoware stack. 
The results demonstrate the feasibility of geometry-based motion perception as a 
complementary approach in autonomous driving systems, particularly in fallback or 
low-confidence situations.

The system was designed to fulfill a set of ten technical and functional 
requirements, introduced in Chapter~\ref{sec:RequirementsForTheThesis}. These 
requirements covered aspects such as stereo-only sensing, semantic independence, 
real-time operation, modular implementation in ROS2, robustness to occlusion, 
and online estimation of 6D motion and criticality metrics.

Almost all design goals were met and validated experimentally. The resulting pipeline relies 
entirely on stereo vision and does not depend on class labels or neural 
networks. Real-time constraints were satisfied on both the development platform 
and the EDGAR research vehicle. Temporal consistency and clustering were 
achieved using recursive Kalman filtering and DBSCAN-based grouping. Criticality 
estimation was performed online using a dedicated time-to-collision module, and 
the entire system was deployed in containerized form.

The only partially fulfilled requirement was the compensation of ego-motion. 
Although a mechanism was implemented using visual odometry, the 
remaining inaccuracies from limitations in the optical flow 
estimation or from the way ego-motion is integrated in the Kalman filter. These 
artefacts can occasionally lead to false positives in cluttered or highly 
dynamic scenes. 

Despite the successful implementation and deployment of the proposed system, 
several limitations were identified during the experimental phase. The most 
notable issue, as mentioned before, is the imperfect compensation of ego-motion, which can lead 
to static objects being erroneously detected as moving when the vehicle is in 
motion. This behavior was particularly evident during turns or accelerations and 
is likely caused by inaccuracies in optical flow estimation, as well as the 
latency or limitations in how the Kalman filter integrates ego-motion updates.

Another aspect requiring attention is the scalability of the Kalman filter. While 
the current implementation achieves real-time performance for a moderate number 
of tracked points, increasing the density results in an exponential growth of 
computation time. Optimizing this module for GPU execution would allow higher 
spatial resolution without sacrificing speed.

To address these limitations and extend the capabilities of the system, the 
following directions are proposed for future work:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Augment the optical flow module:} The current implementation 
      uses the Farnebäck algorithm, which provides reliable performance 
      in well-textured regions but degrades under strong illumination 
      changes or in areas with low visual detail. To address these 
      limitations, future work could integrate learning-based optical 
      flow predictions from RAFT~\cite{optical_flw_raft}, executed in 
      real time and fused with the deterministic estimates. A confidence-
      based fusion strategy could preserve the robustness and 
      interpretability of the current method while leveraging the 
      improved accuracy of deep models in challenging scenes.

  \item \textbf{Improve the visual odometry:} The current module relies on 
      \gls{ORB} feature matching and \gls{RANSAC}-based pose estimation. It achieves 
      good performance in frame-to-frame tracking but accumulates drift 
      over longer sequences and remains sensitive to abrupt motion. As a 
      potential improvement, the integration of MAC-\gls{VO}~\cite{macvo}, a 
      learning-based stereo visual odometry method with covariance 
      prediction, could enhance robustness and long-term consistency. 
      Given its promising formulation, it would be worthwhile to evaluate 
      its suitability for the current pipeline.

  \item \textbf{Accelerate the Kalman filter on GPU:} The current tracking 
      framework executes a separate Kalman filter for each world point, 
      parallelized using multithreading on the CPU. While this setup performs 
      well for medium point densities (e.g., grid spacing of 15 pixels), it 
      does not scale efficiently for denser configurations. Offloading the 
      prediction and update steps to the GPU via CUDA could enable a 
      significant increase in spatial resolution without compromising real-time 
      performance. A possible starting point would be the GPU-based approach 
      presented in~\cite{previous_tfm_gpu_implementation}.  

  \item \textbf{Refine Kalman filter initialization and logic:} The current 
      strategy initializes one Kalman tracker per grid cell, with a fixed 
      spacing that ensures uniform coverage across the image. Although 
      simple and effective, this approach does not take into account which 
      regions of the image are more suitable for tracking. A promising 
      extension, inspired by the Daimler 6D Vision system~\cite{6d_vision_daimler}, 
      would be to initialize multiple filters per location, each with 
      different velocity hypotheses. This could improve robustness in areas 
      with ambiguous or noisy motion. Additionally, the selection of 
      initialization points could be guided by local image properties. 
      For example, filters could be concentrated in regions with high edge 
      content or rich texture, such as object contours or visually structured 
      surfaces, while flat areas like the sky or uniform road patches would 
      be avoided. These properties can be detected using standard techniques 
      such as image gradients or corner detectors, without requiring full 
      motion segmentation, which remains challenging in a moving frame 
      of reference.

      Furthermore, the logic that governs which points are used for downstream 
      tasks could be refined by considering the age of each tracker. 
      For instance, very recent \texttt{WorldPoint} instances might be excluded 
      from output until the underlying filter has had time to converge. This 
      would help reduce the influence of unstable measurements on later stages 
      such as clustering or criticality analysis. 

  
  \item \textbf{Introduce temporal tracking of object clusters:} While the 
      underlying 6D points are tracked across multiple frames via individual 
      Kalman filters, the clustering of these points into object-level 
      representations is currently performed independently at each frame. 
      This frame-by-frame approach does not preserve object identities 
      over time, making it difficult to reason about long-term behavior 
      or motion history. A promising extension would be to implement a 
      temporal tracking mechanism for clusters, assigning consistent IDs 
      to objects as they evolve through the scene. Several strategies could 
      be investigated. For example, clusters in consecutive frames could be 
      matched based on the distance and velocity difference between their 
      centroids, using gating techniques similar to multi-object tracking. 
      Alternatively, cluster correspondence could be established by 
      computing the overlap of their constituent 6D points, leveraging the 
      fact that individual points are already being tracked. These approaches 
      would enable more coherent temporal reasoning and open the door to 
      higher-level tasks such as maneuver classification or intent prediction.

  \item \textbf{Enhance TTC computation:} The current module could be extended 
      by incorporating object trajectory prediction and estimating whether 
      these predicted paths intersect with the ego-vehicle's planned 
      trajectory. This would allow the system to distinguish between 
      actual collision risks and cases of parallel motion. Furthermore, 
      the bounding boxes computed by the object detector could be leveraged 
      to improve collision estimation. Since position, size, and orientation 
      information is already available, these boxes could be propagated 
      along predicted trajectories to perform geometric overlap checks 
      over time. This would provide a more realistic estimate of collision 
      likelihood compared to point-based proximity alone. Similar ideas 
      have been proposed in~\cite{improved_ttc} and could be integrated 
      into the existing framework.

  \item \textbf{Expand camera coverage:} The current configuration uses a single 
      front-facing stereo camera, which limits perception to the forward 
      field of view. Incorporating additional stereo cameras with lateral 
      or rear-facing orientations would enable wider scene coverage and 
      support detection of objects approaching from multiple directions. 
      This would be particularly useful in dynamic environments such as 
      intersections or merging lanes. Moreover, it would facilitate a more 
      meaningful comparison with Autoware, whose 360-degree perception is 
      achieved through LiDAR units mounted in all directions. To support 
      multi-camera operation, an additional package could be added to the 
      perception pipeline to fuse outputs from different object detector 
      nodes, transforming their results into a common frame using the TF 
      tree of the platform. A more advanced extension could involve merging the underlying 
      6D vision images from each camera into a unified spatial map, 
      effectively performing geometric stitching across views. Given the 
      modularity of the current architecture, such additions would be 
      technically feasible and align with the philosophy of the system.


\end{enumerate}


These results demonstrate the feasibility of a deterministic, stereo-only 
perception pipeline for short-term motion understanding and risk assessment. 
While further improvements are possible, the presented system provides a solid 
foundation for future extensions and improvement of the pipeline.
